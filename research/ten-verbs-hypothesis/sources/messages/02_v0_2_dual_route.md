Yes. And I think this brings us very close to the core mechanism of the Ten Verbs Hypothesis.

**There is one reality, potentially many epistemic commons, and many individual world models.** Logical incompatibilities can arise within and between all these representations.

But because they refer to the same reality, incompatibility is meaningful: mutually contradictory claims cannot all be true under the same definitions and conditions.

The question becomes: **How can an intelligence discover that its own model is wrong?**

I see two principal routes in your formulation.

## **1. Two routes to correction**

## **Reality (R)**

The common constraint on every model

Route A

Observation and testing

Route B

Other intelligences and evidence

**Direct encounter**

An intelligence tests its expectations and encounters contradictory evidence

**Mediated correction**

Another intelligence or commons provides evidence, criticism or a new method

**Individual world-model revision**

A correction may be accepted, rejected, or remain unresolved

### **Route A — Reality challenges the individual**

I believe something. I act on it. The consequences contradict my expectations.

Reality constrains what happens regardless of what I believe.

But a qualification matters: reality does not literally tell us which explanation is correct. We interpret observations through instruments and existing models. A failed prediction can reveal an error without identifying its cause.

### **Route B — Another intelligence challenges the individual**

I believe something. Another intelligence holds an incompatible view.

Neither can establish correctness simply by insisting, but the other may possess information, evidence or methods I lack.

Through an epistemic commons, we can compare our claims, investigate the evidence and potentially discover an error.

This route can work across multiple commons.

For example, scientific communities can hold competing theories, while an external experiment or an independently developed method provides a bridge between them.

**The key is that another intelligence can reveal a mistake that my own methods cannot detect.**

And importantly, the two routes are connected. Social correction ultimately draws its epistemic authority from evidence, logic and justified reasoning—not from the number of intelligences who agree.

## **2. The epistemic commons forms a network of networks**

An individual may participate in multiple commons simultaneously: a family, a scientific discipline, a professional organization, a legal system, or a cultural community.

Each commons can contain conflicting interpretations. Different commons can also maintain incompatible accounts of the same events.

But none is reality itself.

This creates an important possibility: **a conflict that cannot be resolved within one commons may be examined through another commons with different information, procedures, or methods.**

The wider network provides additional routes to correction.

It does not guarantee they will work. Several commons may reproduce the same error, and some disagreements may not have an available decisive test.

## **3. The central hypothesis becomes sharper**

I would now formulate this as a foundational mechanism:

### The Dual-Route Correction Hypothesis

Individual intelligences maintain incomplete world models of a shared reality. Through their interactions, they create and participate in multiple, potentially overlapping epistemic commons.

Individual world models and epistemic commons can contain logically incompatible claims. Recognizing such incompatibilities establishes that the relevant claims cannot all be correct, but does not by itself identify the error.

An intelligence has two principal routes to discovering errors in its world model:

**1. Reality-mediated correction:** Through observation, experimentation, and the consequences of action, an intelligence encounters evidence that challenges its existing model.

**2. Commons-mediated correction:** Through interaction with other intelligences and access to their records, arguments, evidence, and methods, an intelligence encounters challenges or possibilities of correction that its own model and methods might not generate.

The Ten Verbs Hypothesis proposes that maintaining the functional capacities represented by the ten verbs supports both routes.

When direct correction is insufficient, additional independent intelligences and epistemic commons may provide alternative routes to identifying errors.

When these corrective routes are persistently obstructed, incompatible claims may remain unresolved and, where they concern shared decisions or expectations, undermine cooperation.

**Prediction:** Under specified conditions, access to additional, sufficiently independent and reliable corrective routes will improve error detection and correction compared with reliance on a restricted set of methods or sources.

This does not imply that more communication, greater consensus, or more participants necessarily improves accuracy.

## **4. One distinction I think we should preserve**

There are actually two separate achievements:

**Recognizing a contradiction** and **discovering what is wrong**.

Suppose two intelligences disagree about \\(P\\).

\\[ M_A \models P,\qquad M_B \models \neg P \\]

Once they establish that they mean the same thing by \\(P\\), they can agree:

\\[ \neg(P\land\neg P) \\]

They now share a valid logical constraint.

But that alone does not establish \\(P\\) or \\(\neg P\\).

For this they need additional grounds. Sometimes a decisive test is available. Sometimes not.

Even when the substantive disagreement remains unresolved, the intelligences can make progress by clarifying definitions, recording evidence, discovering why their conclusions differ, or establishing procedures for joint action.

That is not the same as correcting a factual error, but it can still improve the commons.

## **5. What I think is emerging**

There is a compelling symmetry here:

- Reality can correct an individual.
- Another intelligence can help correct an individual.
- An individual can correct an epistemic commons.
- One epistemic commons can help correct another.

But the final authority is not the individual or the commons. It is what can be justified about reality.

And the learning functions operate across these levels.

I think this gives us a particularly strong formulation:

**The Ten Verbs Hypothesis concerns the conditions under which fallible intelligences can maintain and expand their routes to correction, both through encounters with reality and through the epistemic commons they create together.**

Its relational consequence follows as a separate, testable claim: when consequential disagreements become insulated from correction, cooperation may deteriorate; alternative reliable correction routes may help restore it.

This also connects to your practical motivation. When two individuals cannot establish a workable shared account directly, they can turn to evidence, other intelligences, and institutional procedures. Those may establish facts, clarify remaining uncertainty, or enable decisions without requiring both individuals to adopt the same worldview.

**The commons is not what makes something true. It is what can make the truth discoverable beyond the limits of any one intelligence.**

That may be the most important sentence we've arrived at so far.
