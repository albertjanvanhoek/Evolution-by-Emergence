This second adversarial review forces an important refinement. I accept its main criticism, but I disagree with one of its proposed solutions.

**The hypothesis should not be about how many routes to correction exist. It should be about how intelligences distinguish reliable corrections from errors transmitted through those same routes.**

That is especially important for your original problem: two intelligences can maintain mutually incompatible accounts of a shared situation, and a wider epistemic commons may help them investigate those accounts. But the commons can also reinforce false beliefs, spread misleading information, or become dominated by one interpretation.

The existence of a commons is therefore not enough. Its **capacity to detect and correct errors without amplifying them** becomes central.

I would make three decisions before reformulating the hypothesis.

1. **Retain three ways of discovering problems:** observation of reality, interaction with other intelligences, and examination of one's own reasoning. But these are overlapping checking processes, not three independent sources of truth. Logic can expose a contradiction without establishing which premise is false.
2. **Measure reliability and independence separately from correction outcomes:** source accuracy, shared dependencies, provenance, and the way information is evaluated should be observable before measuring whether a subsequent correction succeeds.
3. **Keep the ten verbs at the centre:** they should describe the candidate functions that enable these checking processes, rather than being added afterward as a moral vocabulary.

There is a scientific precedent for this. Condorcet-style results depend critically on the accuracy and conditional independence of judgments. Zollman's network-epistemology models demonstrate trade-offs between communication, speed, and reliability. And the literature on independent evidence warns that apparent robustness can be misleading when different methods share assumptions or errors.&#x20;

One qualification to the review: adding independent routes does **not** necessarily improve accuracy. Independence alone is insufficient. A new route may be systematically misleading, irrelevant, or badly interpreted.

So the prediction needs to concern both the information contained in the route and the way an intelligence evaluates it.

## **The Ten Verbs Hypothesis — version 0.3**

# The Ten Verbs Hypothesis

### Error detection, correction, and cooperation among learning intelligences

**Status:** Proposed theoretical and empirical hypothesis.

### 1. Foundational model

Interacting intelligences exist within a shared reality (R). Each maintains an incomplete and fallible world model (Mᵢ).

Through their interactions, intelligences establish multiple, potentially overlapping epistemic commons (C₁, C₂, …).

An epistemic commons is not a collective mind. It consists of information, representations, methods, and records made accessible between intelligences, including through durable external media.

Reality is not identical to any individual model or commons. Some social facts are constituted partly through shared representations and collective actions, but no individual can make an independently testable proposition true merely by insisting upon it.

A commons is neither necessarily correct nor internally consistent. It can contain contradictory claims, distorted evidence, and mutually incompatible methods.

### 2. Logical disagreement

Two intelligences disagree logically when they hold incompatible propositions about the same subject under the same definitions, time, and scope.

If A believes P and B believes not-P, at least one belief is false under classical logic.

However, identifying the incompatibility does not establish which belief is false.

This distinction is central: **detecting a contradiction is not equivalent to identifying the error.**

Disagreements over values, preferences, or interests need separate treatment, because they need not have a uniquely correct factual resolution.

### 3. Three ways of discovering problems

An intelligence can identify possible problems in its world model through three interacting processes.

**Reality-mediated checking:** Observation, experimentation, and consequences can challenge predictions. This depends on the availability, accuracy, timeliness, and interpretability of feedback.

**Reasoning-mediated checking:** Logical analysis can reveal contradictions, invalid inferences, or inconsistencies within a model. This does not necessarily identify which premise is false.

**Commons-mediated checking:** Other intelligences can provide observations, testimony, criticism, records, or methods unavailable to the individual.

These processes overlap. Evidence obtained from others ultimately depends on their encounters with reality and their methods of interpretation. Reasoning also depends on representations acquired through previous experience.

None guarantees correction. The same processes can introduce, reproduce, or amplify errors.

### 4. Central hypothesis: corrective filtering

**The Ten Verbs Hypothesis proposes that learning intelligences maintain more accurate and correctable world models when their learning practices enable them to detect genuine errors, evaluate corrective information, reject misleading information, and preserve or develop reliable methods of inquiry.**

The ten verbs are proposed functional components of this capacity.

The empirical prediction is not that more information or more correction attempts automatically improve learning.

Rather, **the effectiveness of correction depends on the informativeness of observations, the reliability and error dependence of sources, and the capacity of the receiving intelligence to evaluate and use those signals.**

Access to additional sources that reproduce the same error may increase confidence without improving accuracy.

### 5. The ten functional components

| Verb                | Proposed measurable function                                        |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Stay open           | Consider credible alternatives and disconfirming evidence           |
| Try                 | Generate informative tests or actions                               |
| Notice              | Distinguish observations from expectations                          |
| Be honest           | Preserve evidence fidelity, internally and when communicating       |
| Change              | Revise beliefs appropriately when evidence changes                  |
| Remember and let go | Preserve warranted learning, retract errors, and accommodate change |
| Take care           | Sustain the resources, safety, and conditions needed for inquiry    |
| Share               | Exchange information while preserving accuracy and provenance       |
| Repair              | Restore useful correction pathways when appropriate and safe        |
| Find new ways       | Develop, acquire, compare, and validate methods of inquiry          |

These components may interact, overlap, or turn out to be reducible to fewer functions.

The hypothesis does not assume that all ten are universally necessary or that their names uniquely describe learning.

### 6. Relational hypothesis

When intelligences maintain incompatible accounts of consequential shared events, their ability to cooperate depends partly on whether they can identify and manage these incompatibilities.

The hypothesis predicts that persistent obstruction of effective corrective processes will increase the risk of unresolved factual disagreement and impaired coordination in relationships where accurate shared understanding matters for joint action.

A wider epistemic commons may provide additional evidence, independent perspectives, or methods when direct correction between two intelligences fails.

However, commons-mediated interaction can also amplify false claims or increase conflict. Its value depends on how information is evaluated, not simply whether additional participants become involved.

**Epistemic correction and relationship persistence are separate outcomes.**

A correction may improve accuracy while reducing trust or cohesion. A relationship may persist despite mutually reinforced false beliefs.

Resolution may involve correction, acknowledgment of uncertainty, agreement on procedures, or practical arrangements that do not require identical world models.

### 7. Protection against contamination

The same routes that transmit correction can transmit misinformation and error.

Effective learning therefore requires discrimination between credible and misleading signals.

In adversarial settings, the maintenance of a commons may require accountability, restrictions on information access, independent verification, and boundaries against manipulation.

Repair does not imply unconditional reconciliation. Sharing does not imply indiscriminate disclosure. Openness does not imply accepting all claims.

The proposed function of these practices is to protect opportunities for reliable correction while limiting error propagation.

### 8. Testable predictions

**H1 — Error dependence.** In environments with independently verifiable outcomes, agents exposed to informative signals generated through distinct error-producing mechanisms will, under an appropriately specified evidence-integration procedure, achieve better calibrated predictions than agents exposed to equivalent numbers of duplicated signals.

**H2 — Provenance-aware filtering.** When sources vary in reliability and dependence, agents provided with accurate source-provenance information and an effective evaluation procedure will accept fewer false corrections and exhibit less confidence inflation than agents relying only on the number of agreeing messages.

**H3 — Functional differentiation.** Selectively impairing individual learning functions will produce distinguishable patterns of impairment across reality-mediated, reasoning-mediated, and commons-mediated checking. These predictions must be specified for each task before testing.

**H4 — Relational correctability.** In collaborative tasks involving consequential factual disagreement and compatible goals, independently measured corrective practices will predict subsequent error resolution and cooperative performance beyond initial agreement, communication volume, and interpersonal warmth.

**H5 — Trade-offs.** Where factual correction threatens identity, interests, or group membership, improvements in epistemic accuracy need not improve immediate relational satisfaction. The direction and magnitude of the relationship effect are expected to depend on these prespecified conditions.

### 9. What would count against it?

A primary test would compare provenance-aware correction procedures with communication-matched controls in tasks containing both independently generated evidence and duplicated or misleading evidence.

Before data collection, the study would specify meaningful effect sizes, the expected interactions between source dependence and correction procedure, and the criteria for successful manipulation.

The central empirical claim would be weakened if the intervention reliably changed correction practices but produced no meaningful improvement in accuracy, error rejection, or calibration, with estimates sufficiently precise to exclude the prespecified effects.

The proposed ten-component decomposition would be weakened if a simpler model explained the same behavioural data equally well or better in independent validation.

The relational extension would be weakened if correction practices contributed no predictive or causal information about handling consequential disagreement beyond established relational and task variables.

Negative evidence against one component does not establish that every other component is false. But the theory must not be protected by inventing new exceptions after observing an unwelcome result.

### 10. The central question

**Under what conditions can fallible intelligences detect and correct errors in their individual and shared representations of reality, while preserving the capacity to cooperate despite unresolved differences?**

The Ten Verbs Hypothesis proposes a functional vocabulary for investigating that question. Whether those ten functions form a useful, generalizable explanatory model remains to be demonstrated.

## **Two points I would challenge in the adversarial review**

First, I would resist treating logic as a third independent *source of truth*. It is a third way to check a model, but it operates on representations and premises. A consistent model can be false, and an inconsistent model can contain some true statements. Logical checking and empirical checking therefore answer different questions.

Second, the review's suggested falsifier—adding independent routes must improve correction—is too strong without additional assumptions. The independence of errors is not sufficient when the evidence itself is uninformative or systematically misleading. Nor should an additional route be expected to help if the recipient cannot interpret it.

The stronger empirical question is whether learning agents use **measurable signal reliability, provenance, and dependence** in ways that improve subsequent accuracy. We can test this without defining successful correction into the intervention.

## **How this connects to the existing literature**

The revised hypothesis sits at the intersection of several established ideas.

Peirce and Longino: inquiry must remain answerable to something beyond individual conviction, and criticism between individuals plays a role in producing and evaluating knowledge. Their work provides philosophical foundations for the commons, not independent evidence that the ten verbs form the right decomposition.&#x20;

## **One remaining conceptual decision**

I would now stop adding mechanisms to the hypothesis and focus on one boundary.

**The epistemic commons is not simply an information network. It is an information network between intelligences that can interpret, challenge, and potentially correct one another.**

Two commons can overlap without sharing all their beliefs or methods. For empirical work, we can identify a particular commons through its participants, accessible records, communication links, and practices for evaluating claims. Its boundaries may be porous and change over time.

What makes the hypothesis distinctive is the proposal that *maintaining such a network's correctability* matters even when agreement is impossible.

And this returns to the insight that motivated the entire discussion: two people may have incompatible accounts of shared events, and direct communication may fail. A wider commons can sometimes provide another way to examine those accounts.

But that does not guarantee agreement, correction, or reconciliation.

It provides **an additional opportunity for learning whose reliability must itself be evaluated**.

I think that is the defensible centre of the theory. It retains the practical meaning of the ten verbs while allowing scientific inquiry to discover that some verbs are redundant, some are conditional, or some have effects opposite to those initially expected.
