# Distribution-First Economics
## Separating the Cost of Access from the Reward for Innovation

### 1. The central proposition

Modern economies often reward the creation of valuable products by charging users more than the cost of supplying them. This can finance innovation, compensate investors and encourage efficient production. But it can also restrict access to solutions that already exist, especially when the cost of serving an additional user is low.

**The central proposition of distribution-first economics is that the reward for creating a solution need not always be collected through the price of accessing that solution.**

Where practical, the economy can separate four functions:

1. **Discovery and allocation:** determining what people value, which resources are scarce and what should be produced.
2. **Production and cost recovery:** paying for the resources required to supply, maintain and renew a solution.
3. **Innovation reward:** compensating those who create valuable improvements.
4. **Distribution:** enabling beneficial access while deciding transparently who pays for shared costs and subsidies.

These functions are related, but they do not necessarily require one price to perform all four tasks.

The proposal is not to abolish markets or profits. It is to create an additional economic architecture in which **access can approach efficient supply cost while innovation is rewarded through a separate, explicit mechanism**.

### 2. The objective: maximise beneficial distribution, not simply minimise prices

The goal is not to make everything free or to produce as much as possible.

It is to maximise the realised social value of useful solutions, subject to real resource constraints, environmental limits, sustainable financing and continued innovation.

A lower price is desirable when it allows additional beneficial use without imposing greater costs elsewhere. But low prices can be harmful when they encourage waste, overwhelm capacity or conceal genuine scarcity.

Distribution-first economics therefore distinguishes three situations:

- **Non-rival or low-marginal-cost goods**, such as software, knowledge and certain digital services, where broad access may be possible at very low additional cost.
- **Capacity-constrained services**, such as networks, laboratories and infrastructure, where congestion and expansion costs matter.
- **Rival, resource-intensive goods**, such as medicines, equipment and physical materials, where additional supply consumes scarce inputs.

The appropriate access price will differ across these categories.

The governing principle is **efficient and sustainable access**, not universal zero pricing.

### 3. The access-price layer

Participating providers would disclose and verify the economic costs of supplying their products or services.

These costs would include:

- Direct labour, materials, energy and other inputs.
- Maintenance, quality assurance and necessary administration.
- Depreciation and economically justified replacement of assets.
- Financing costs and appropriate compensation for risk.
- Environmental and other relevant external costs.
- The opportunity cost of genuinely scarce resources.

The accounting must distinguish actual expenditure from **efficient economic cost**. A provider should not be entitled to recover avoidable waste merely because it was incurred.

Where intermediate inputs are supplied by other participating providers, their costs can be traced recursively. Under a simplified fixed-coefficient input-output model:

\[
p = Ap + v
\]

and, if the system is productive and the relevant inverse exists:

\[
p = (I-A)^{-1}v
\]

Here, \(A\) describes intermediate input requirements, while \(v\) represents direct economic costs.

This model makes upstream costs traceable and can reveal where successive margins accumulate. It does not, by itself, determine what should be produced, establish efficient input quantities or replace information about demand and scarcity.

**Recursive cost transparency is therefore an accounting and coordination instrument, not a substitute for market discovery.**

Providers would remain subject to competitive comparison, independent benchmarking and performance requirements.

### 4. Shared infrastructure as a separately funded commons

Some costs arise from maintaining a system for everyone rather than serving a particular additional user.

Examples include interoperability standards, shared digital infrastructure, open technical interfaces, safety certification, common billing systems and foundational research tools.

Allocating all these fixed costs to individual transactions can create a problem: higher per-user charges reduce participation, which forces remaining users to carry a larger share of the fixed costs.

Where there is a credible collective benefit, such infrastructure could instead be financed through a common fund.

Possible sources include public budgets, institutional subscriptions, sector contributions, procurement contracts or negotiated beneficiary payments.

This would allow usage charges to reflect the additional cost of usage more closely, while keeping fixed infrastructure adequately funded.

However, collective funding would be justified only where shared provision demonstrably outperforms feasible alternatives. Governance must prevent the commons from becoming an unaccountable monopoly.

### 5. Innovation rewards outside the access price

The most distinctive part of the proposal is a separate **innovation reward layer**.

Instead of relying exclusively on a markup charged to every future user, innovators could receive explicit compensation for producing verified value.

This compensation could take several forms:

- **Prospective prizes:** rewards announced for solving clearly specified problems.
- **Milestone funding:** staged payments for demonstrated technical progress.
- **Outcome bounties:** rewards linked to independently verified results.
- **Shared-savings contracts:** a defined share of cost reductions attributable to an innovation.
- **Advance purchase commitments:** guaranteed demand for solutions meeting agreed criteria.

The reward should not be calculated solely from download counts, sales volume or monetary willingness to pay.

These measures can be misleading, particularly when access is inexpensive or beneficiaries have little purchasing power.

Instead, the system would evaluate a combination of:

1. **Additionality:** what improvement occurred compared with a credible alternative?
2. **Beneficial use:** how many people or institutions actually gained value?
3. **Outcome quality:** what measurable improvements resulted?
4. **Resource efficiency:** what costs, risks or environmental burdens were avoided?
5. **Uncertainty and risk:** what investment and probability of failure did the innovator bear?

Different sectors would require different evaluation methods. Health outcomes cannot be measured in the same way as software reliability or energy savings.

There is no universal formula that can objectively measure all social value. Consequently, bounty-setting is an institutional design problem involving evidence, judgement, accountability and periodic revision.

### 6. Preserving incentives for efficiency

A critical danger of at-cost systems is that reimbursing expenditure can weaken the incentive to reduce it.

Distribution-first economics should therefore **not** promise automatic reimbursement of whatever a provider spends.

Instead, providers could operate under a combination of:

- Competitive tenders and supplier choice.
- Published cost benchmarks.
- Multi-year efficiency targets.
- Independent audits.
- Rewards for verified cost reductions.
- Penalties for persistent underperformance.
- Limited periods during which innovators retain a share of the savings they create.

For example, if a provider develops a method that reduces independently verified annual operating costs by €1 million, a contract might award the provider a defined portion of those savings for several years. Users would receive the remaining savings immediately and the full savings after the reward period.

This creates an incentive to innovate without requiring indefinite high access prices.

The baseline against which savings are measured would need independent verification to prevent manipulation.

### 7. Preserving scarcity signals and decentralised discovery

The proposal must take seriously the insight that prices convey dispersed information.

A cost-transparent provider still needs to know whether customers want its product, whether alternative inputs are more valuable elsewhere and whether additional capacity is worth building.

Therefore, distribution-first arrangements should preserve mechanisms such as competitive procurement, user choice, demand measurement and prices that reflect real congestion or scarcity.

A practical access-price rule might be expressed as:

\[
P_{\text{access}}
=
C_{\text{incremental}}
+
C_{\text{scarcity}}
+
C_{\text{externalities}}
-
S_{\text{explicit}}
\]

where:

- \(C_{\text{incremental}}\) is the efficient additional cost of supply.
- \(C_{\text{scarcity}}\) reflects genuine capacity or opportunity costs.
- \(C_{\text{externalities}}\) accounts for costs imposed on others.
- \(S_{\text{explicit}}\) is a transparent, separately financed subsidy.

Long-term fixed costs, innovation rewards and common infrastructure would be financed through explicitly identified mechanisms rather than hidden inside every transaction.

This is a guiding framework, not a universally applicable tariff formula. In some settings, subscriptions, two-part tariffs or conventional market prices will remain more efficient.

The important distinction is between **prices that communicate real resource scarcity** and **markups used primarily to finance innovation or collect economic rents**.

The proposal seeks to separate them where doing so creates a net benefit.

### 8. Governance and protection against capture

Separating innovation rewards from access prices creates new risks.

A bounty fund could be captured by established firms, politically influential organisations or administrators. Innovators could exaggerate outcomes, manipulate baselines or optimise for measured indicators rather than genuine benefit.

The system therefore requires:

- Transparent eligibility and evaluation criteria.
- Independent technical and financial assessment.
- Publication of awards, methods and conflicts of interest.
- Open opportunities for competing solutions.
- Appeals and periodic external review.
- Contractual clawbacks for fraud or materially false claims.
- Clear treatment of intellectual property, licensing and access rights.

Where public funds support an innovation, the funding agreement should specify the corresponding public benefit, such as affordable licensing, interoperability or non-exclusive access.

Bounties must also be financially bounded. An open-ended promise to pay for all claimed social value would be neither credible nor fiscally sustainable.

### 9. A market-compatible transition

Distribution-first economics need not be implemented across an entire economy.

It can begin as a **voluntary institutional alternative** within selected sectors.

Participating providers could offer verified cost-based supply in exchange for access to shared infrastructure, innovation funds, predictable procurement or reduced commercial risk.

Traditional providers would remain free to compete.

This competition would be informative. If distribution-first arrangements deliver lower total costs, greater access and sustained innovation, they may attract users and suppliers. If they underperform, the model should be revised or abandoned.

Such experimentation is preferable to assuming that the architecture will succeed universally.

### 10. A proposed first pilot

A promising initial setting is a shared open-source software service used by universities, hospitals, municipalities or other public institutions.

The pilot could include:

**A shared infrastructure fund** covering interoperability, security standards, core maintenance and essential common services.

**Transparent operating contracts** with independently reviewed costs and competitive performance benchmarks.

**An innovation bounty fund** rewarding verified improvements in reliability, accessibility, security, functionality or operating efficiency.

**A low-friction access model** that permits additional institutions to participate at a price reflecting the real additional cost of serving them, while financing shared costs collectively.

The pilot should be compared with conventional commercial licensing and ordinary public procurement.

Its success criteria should include:

- Total social cost, including administration and governance.
- Number of institutions and users obtaining beneficial access.
- Service quality, security and reliability.
- Rate and quality of innovation.
- Cost reduction over time.
- Financial sustainability and maintenance quality.
- Supplier diversity and freedom to switch providers.

The evaluation should be independently designed and capable of concluding that the proposal does not outperform existing arrangements.

### 11. What the proposal claims—and what it does not

Distribution-first economics does **not** claim that:

- Production costs determine economic value.
- All prices should equal accounting costs.
- All intellectual property rights should be abolished.
- Public authorities can calculate the full value of every innovation.
- Market prices contain no useful information.
- A bounty system will always outperform patents, competition or ordinary procurement.

Its narrower and more defensible claim is that **some of the functions currently bundled into product prices can be separated, and that doing so may increase beneficial access without undermining efficient production or innovation**.

The strongest opportunities are likely to arise where marginal supply costs are low, fixed development costs are high, access restrictions cause substantial social losses and innovation outcomes can be evaluated credibly.

Other sectors may require different arrangements or may be poorly suited to the model.

### 12. The research agenda

The proposal creates five testable questions.

**First:** Under what economic conditions does separating innovation rewards from access prices improve welfare compared with conventional pricing?

**Second:** Can recursive cost transparency reduce cumulative markups and transaction costs without weakening competition or efficiency?

**Third:** Which methods of valuing innovation produce sufficiently accurate, robust and legitimate rewards?

**Fourth:** Which governance structures can finance shared infrastructure while resisting capture, excessive bureaucracy and underinvestment?

**Fifth:** Can the resulting institutions preserve decentralised discovery, efficient resource allocation and the incentives needed for continuous improvement?

These questions require theoretical models, empirical comparisons, legal analysis and real-world pilots.

Formal verification can help establish whether specific conclusions follow from clearly stated assumptions. It cannot establish that those assumptions hold in practice. The empirical and institutional questions must therefore remain open.

### Conclusion

The central economic challenge is not simply to produce more innovations. It is to ensure that valuable innovations are created, maintained, improved **and used by as many people as can benefit from them without imposing greater social costs**.

Conventional pricing often combines resource allocation, cost recovery, innovation incentives and distribution in a single transaction price.

Distribution-first economics proposes that these functions can sometimes be organised separately.

Its central hypothesis is:

**When the cost of extending access is substantially lower than the price required to finance innovation, a transparent combination of efficient access pricing, shared infrastructure funding and independently governed innovation rewards can increase total social welfare—provided that scarcity signals, cost discipline and decentralised discovery are preserved.**

This is not a claim that markets should be replaced.

It is a proposal to expand the institutional possibilities available within a market economy—and to test whether rewarding creation separately from access can allow valuable solutions to reach further.
