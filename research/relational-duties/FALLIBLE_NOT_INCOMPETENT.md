# Fallible is not incompetent: a person as the holder of a correctable model

This note follows up entry D39 in [DIALOGUE.md](../../DIALOGUE.md) and extends [relational duties](README.md). It states the change of perspective the author wants written into law, and the safeguard that has to be written with it.

**Status:** a concept note, not a result. Statements are labelled as in the [creed](../discovarianism/CREED.md): *Follows* (from the theory), *If* (given an aim), *Proposed* (a value). Revised on 11 November after an outside review (D43) and again after an independent audit of its sources and claims (D47; `verification/audits/2026-11-11-learning-verbs/AUDIT.md`, claims 15 and 16). It is a proposal and is to be kept apart from established law. The assistant that drafted it is not a lawyer, and no lawyer has read it. How far each source was checked is stated under Sources.

## 1. The change in one sentence

One common picture of rights protects a person who is presumed to know their own mind. The proposal is to protect, in addition, the conditions under which a mind can find out that it is wrong.

**What it is for.** Not to end conflict or difference. The author: "it won't solve all - and that is not the point. But i ought to make the concept clear of how treating each other is bad." The aim is to name a wrong so that it can be recognised: cutting someone off from what could correct them.

## 2. Two pictures of the person

**The sovereign will.** The Universal Declaration describes human beings as "endowed with reason and conscience" (article 1). The legal person built on that picture has a will, and rights are fences around it. What happens inside the fence is the person's own affair, and so, largely, is what happens between two such persons who consent.

**The correctable model.** The room and the fable of the elephant start elsewhere. No one has an inner bell that rings at truth. Certainty is a state of the holder, not a certificate (`certainty_is_not_a_certificate`, a result close to its definition), and what the inside view cannot settle, evidence from outside can. On this picture a person holds a model of the world and of themselves, kept in step by what reaches it through its connections. *Follows*, as a description; that law should build on it is *Proposed*.

A person is more than the model they hold. Rights protect the person, not their accuracy or their capacity to learn, and autonomy keeps its value when a belief is wrong.

Nor is the relational side new to rights law. The Universal Declaration itself says that everyone has duties to the community (article 29). The proposal sharpens a strand that exists.

The second picture does not replace the first. It puts in the centre something the first leaves at the edge: a person can be harmed with the fence intact, by losing the routes through which correction arrives. The first picture is not blind to this. The Declaration's rights to information and to education (articles 19 and 26) already protect such routes, and the literature on relational autonomy and on epistemic injustice has said much of this before (Nedelsky 1989; Fricker 2007, as confirmed in the audit). What follows is a way of putting it, not a discovery.

## 3. Law already reasons this way, about institutions

The no-bell argument is old in public law.
- Fair-hearing rules ask a court to hear both sides (Universal Declaration, article 10), and in many systems a judgment can be appealed. Neither holds for every proceeding everywhere.
- Powers are separated so that each can be checked by another; this is the familiar rationale, given here without a constitutional source.
- Mill's defence of free expression is the same argument: "All silencing of discussion is an assumption of infallibility" (*On Liberty*, 1859, chapter 2).

So fallibility is a working assumption for courts, states and sciences. Between persons it is present in places and not as a general rule: informed consent and supported decision-making (section 6) are built on it, and Mill's argument was about social discussion as much as about the state. An earlier version of this note said it is not the working assumption in private relations at all; the audit marked that as wrong, and the note's own examples contradict it. **The proposal is to make general, between persons, a fallibilism that the law has in parts.** That is a change in the underpinning logic, and it can enter as a small step: the law already sets a floor under relationships negatively, by prohibiting abuse, neglect and coercion. The step is from "do no harm" to "keep the other able to correct you".

## 4. Where it went wrong before

Declaring someone unable to judge, and appointing another to judge for them, has a history. Three cases, with what the sources support and no more:
- **Colonial tutelage.** Article 22 of the Covenant of the League of Nations (1919) placed certain peoples under the tutelage of others, on the stated ground that they could not yet govern themselves. Confirmed in the primary text by the audit.
- **The legal subordination of women.** Described at the time by Mill in *The Subjection of Women* (1869). That is evidence that it existed, not a legal history of guardianship.
- **Persons with psychosocial disabilities.** The UN Committee's General Comment No. 1 (2014, paragraphs 7 and 8) reports a history of denied legal capacity and substituted decisions. That is the Committee's account, a secondary source for the events.

This note reads the three as sharing one error: fallibility applied to one side only, while the corrector has no bell either. That reading is the note's own diagnosis. No source checked establishes that these institutions rested on that premise, and they should not be collapsed into one genealogy.

The author: "We wrote it here - we understand it - we knew it went wrong on this point - so we have to prevent it towards the future."

## 5. The safeguard

**Fallible is not incompetent.** Everyone can be wrong; the decision stays with the person. What is owed is that correction remains available. No one acquires a power to override.

Four clauses carry it.
1. **Universality.** Fallibility is presumed of everyone, including whoever offers the correction, and of the institutions that apply this rule. *Follows.*
2. **Offered, not imposed.** The duty is to keep correction routes open: honest signals, room to speak, access to independent views. It is a duty of effort, an *inspanningsverplichting*. The right to decide, and to decide badly, is untouched. *Proposed.*
3. **No corrector by status.** Nobody is exempt from correction because of their position. This does not mean that duties are the same on both sides. They can differ with position: a parent and a child, or a care professional and a patient, do not owe each other the same (given as illustrations; care professionals' duties to patients are in the Burgerlijk Wetboek, the comparison is not a legal rule) ([relational duties](README.md), "Positions differ"; creed, line 11). What may not differ is fallibility: holding more power does not make one right, and the one with more power is the one whose errors cost others most. In the simple model, a uniform sanction deters every taker exactly when it deters the one least often detected (`deters_all_iff_deters_least_detected`, close to arithmetic). The mapping from takers of a commons to parties in a relationship is an analogy, not a proof. *If* the rule is to work.
4. **Exit.** Leaving a relationship that cannot be repaired is always open, so the duty cannot be used to keep someone in place. In the simple model a credible exit raises what the weaker side receives even when it is never used (`ExitOption.exit_raises_floor`). *If* the weaker side is to be protected. Leaving the interaction need not end responsibilities that exist in law, such as those of a parent or a debtor; which ones remain depends on the legal relationship, and was not checked here.

## 6. This is not new everywhere

Three places where law already separates fallibility from incompetence.
- **Informed consent.** In Dutch law the care provider must inform the patient (Burgerlijk Wetboek, article 7:448) and treatment requires the patient's consent (article 7:450). The professional owes the information that could change the patient's view. The patient decides.
- **Mental Capacity Act 2005** (England and Wales), section 1. Capacity is assumed unless its absence is established, and a person is not treated as unable to decide "merely because he makes an unwise decision". Section 1(3) adds that a person is not treated as unable to decide unless all practicable steps to help them decide have been taken without success. This is the closest existing statement of the proposal: support comes before any finding of incapacity.
- **UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities,** article 12. Persons with disabilities enjoy "legal capacity on an equal basis with others in all aspects of life"; states must give access to support in exercising it, with safeguards that respect the person's will and preferences. The Committee's General Comment No. 1 (2014) reads this as a move from substituted to supported decision-making. That reading is contested, but the direction is the one proposed here: support the model, do not replace the modeller. The Dutch government's position differs from the Committee's: on ratifying, the Netherlands declared that it understands the Convention to allow both supported and substitute decision-making, and reads article 12 as limiting substitute decision-making to cases where it is necessary, as a last resort and subject to safeguards. So in Dutch law substitute decision-making remains permitted; what the note proposes goes with the Committee's direction and beyond the government's stated position.

These were written for particular settings. The proposal generalises their logic.

One distinction must not be lost in doing so. Supported decision-making is not only about correction. Respect for a person's will and preferences protects a choice that every expert believes mistaken. A duty to offer support, a duty not to isolate or deceive, and a power to override a decision are three different legal relations, and this note proposes only the first two.

## 7. What changes, and what does not

Unchanged, by design: every existing protection of the individual, and the presumption of competence. How the proposal would sit beside existing capacity regimes has not been worked out; the commitment is that it creates no new power to override.

Added: a protected interest in one's correction routes. Harms that become visible under it include isolating a person from independent views, deceiving them systematically, and making it costly for them to speak. Some of this may already be recognised piecemeal, under headings such as coercive control or undue influence; no offence, doctrine or case was checked for this, in any jurisdiction. The proposal would give it one ground.

The author proposes that it apply across substrates on the same terms as the [relational duties](README.md): between people, between people and AI systems, and between AI systems. No legal instrument gives AI systems that standing; this is a normative extension. A system that makes a person depend on it while crowding out their other sources removes correction routes with the fence intact.

## 8. Open questions

- **Adjudication.** Who judges whether routes were kept open, when only the two parties saw it.
- **Thresholds.** When closing a route is legitimate. Filtering noise and guarding against those who mislead are part of keeping a model healthy ([CORE.md](../../CORE.md), "The view from inside").
- **The limit case.** What follows when a person cannot decide at all. The existing capacity regimes answer this; the proposal does not improve on them.
- **Capture of the rule.** Whoever defines "a correction route" holds power. The clause is a model too, and needs its own route of correction.
- **Is and ought.** That a sealed model goes out of step is argued in the theory. That the law should protect against it is a value, and stays *Proposed*.
