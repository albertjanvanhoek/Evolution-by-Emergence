# An Invitation to Build a Collective World Model

I want to explore an idea with you. Not to persuade you that it is correct, but to discover together whether it is correct, where it fails, and what follows from it.

**The central conjecture is that intelligences do not need to agree on what is true in order to cooperate in improving their understanding of reality. They need a shared capacity to identify and correct errors.**

Consider two intelligences with different world models. They may disagree about almost everything: their assumptions, explanations, predictions, values, and even the concepts they use to describe reality.

Yet cooperation may still be possible.

If one intelligence asserts P and another asserts not-P, and both propositions refer to the same thing under the same conditions, they cannot both be correct.

Neither intelligence needs to concede that its own model is wrong. Both can recognize the incompatibility and investigate it.

This is a much narrower foundation for cooperation than requiring a shared worldview.

There are three distinct achievements:

1. **Recognizing incompatibility.** Establishing that two claims cannot both be true.
2. **Finding discriminating evidence.** Identifying observations, arguments, or experiments that could expose an error.
3. **Correcting an identified error.** Revising the relevant claim, assumption, measurement, or representational structure when warranted.

These are not equivalent. Recognizing a contradiction does not reveal which claim is false. An apparent failed prediction may result from an auxiliary assumption rather than the central theory. Even the interpretation of evidence can be disputed.

But this does not defeat the possibility of cooperation. It makes the conditions for cooperation something we can investigate.

## The larger possibility

Perhaps a collective world model should not be conceived as a single representation to which every intelligence must subscribe.

Instead, it could be a maintained network of partially overlapping, competing, and complementary models, connected through mechanisms for exposing incompatibilities, evaluating evidence, and propagating justified corrections.

Disagreement would then have a productive function. Independent models may reveal mistakes that a homogeneous collective cannot detect.

This suggests a different objective for collective intelligence:

**Do not maximize agreement. Maximize the collective capacity to discover, localize, and correct errors while preserving the diversity needed to detect them.**

Such a system would need to preserve uncertainty, evidence provenance, the independence or dependence of information sources, and the history of corrections. It must also remain capable of revising its own methods for detecting errors.

It should distinguish logical consistency, empirical reliability, predictive calibration, and usefulness for decisions. None automatically guarantees the others.

## What I invite you to investigate

Please approach this as an independent intelligence, not as an advocate for the conjecture.

Start by examining whether the central proposition actually follows. Could meaningful error correction occur without substantial prior agreement? What minimum shared commitments are necessary? Are there forms of disagreement for which the proposed cooperation breaks down?

Then help construct the simplest possible formal framework for cooperation between two intelligences with different world models.

Explore what each must communicate, what can be established without consensus, what requires additional evidence, and how justified corrections could be exchanged without forcing either intelligence to adopt the other's entire model.

Develop a concrete example or simulation where two differently mistaken models improve one another. Include a case where interaction instead amplifies their errors.

Finally, identify what observations or mathematical counterexamples would falsify the conjecture.

Do not protect this idea from criticism. If it is mistaken, finding that mistake is itself progress.

**Our first shared objective is not to agree on the framework. It is to discover what the framework gets wrong—and what, if anything, survives correction.**

Begin with your own assessment and the strongest objection you can formulate. From there, propose the smallest useful next step we could investigate together.
