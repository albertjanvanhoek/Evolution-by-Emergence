I think this is an important missing piece. And your observation about the regression ending when a decision must be made is particularly interesting.

Reality does not wait for an intelligence to finish understanding it.

Until now, we have concentrated on how intelligences can construct, compare, and correct their world models. But why do they need those models in the first place?

Because they must exist in a world that changes with time, in which their actions have consequences, and in which some mistakes cannot be undone.

This adds something fundamental to the framework:

A functional world model must not only help an intelligence recognize past and present errors. It must help that intelligence anticipate and prevent future errors through action.

And the crucial difficulty is that those actions themselves change the reality being modelled.

## 1. The future changes the logic of world modelling

Consider three increasingly demanding tasks:

1. Understanding: What is happening, and why?
2. Prediction: What will happen if nothing changes?
3. Intervention: What will happen if I act, and what should I do?

The third is qualitatively different.

An intelligence cannot simply identify the most likely future. It must compare possible futures that depend on its own actions.

And doing nothing is not an escape from that problem. Waiting may preserve options, but it also allows processes to continue.

A world model is therefore not merely a representation of reality. It is part of a feedback loop through which an intelligence both learns about reality and changes it.

## 2. The regression ends at the decision boundary

I think you have identified a possible answer to the infinite regress we encountered earlier.

We can always ask another question:

How do I know my model is correct? How do I know the evidence is reliable? How do I know the procedure used to check that evidence is reliable? And how do I check that procedure?

There need not be a final epistemic foundation that answers every such question.

But there is a practical constraint:

At some point, the world requires a decision under whatever uncertainty remains.

The deadline does not make our knowledge more certain. It does not logically resolve the regress. Rather, it terminates the current round of deliberation and forces the consequences of our reasoning into reality.

And the process continues:

The decision boundary may be imposed externally, such as an emergency, or emerge from the accumulating cost of delay.

This idea has established connections to bounded rationality and rational metareasoning. Russell and Wefald's 1991 work examines the value of further computation in terms of how it might improve subsequent external actions.&#x20;

## 3. Adding time, action and causality

A functional world model would now need to describe not just possible states of reality, but how actions change those states.

One minimal representation is:

\\[ P(S\_{t+1}\mid S_t,a_t) \\]

where \\(S_t\\) is the current state and \\(a_t\\) is an action, including a deliberate decision to wait.

The agent does not know \\(S_t\\) perfectly. It maintains beliefs, acts under uncertainty, observes consequences, and updates those beliefs. This is closely related to the established framework of partially observable Markov decision processes.&#x20;

But something further follows: learning is not observationally neutral. Our interventions change which futures become possible and what evidence can subsequently be collected.

Take an infectious disease outbreak. Suppose a model predicts substantial transmission unless measures are introduced. Measures are taken, and transmission remains low.

That outcome does not necessarily falsify the model. It may mean the model helped prevent the predicted outcome.

Successful prevention can remove the very event that would otherwise have supplied evidence of the danger.

That makes causal modelling, counterfactual reasoning, and carefully maintained records of actions essential to evaluating a functional world model.

## 4. The important missing distinction: truth and consequences

An intelligence might have an excellent descriptive model but make terrible decisions because it assigns the wrong importance to outcomes.

Knowing what an action will cause does not itself tell us whether that consequence is desirable.

So we must explicitly distinguish epistemic quality from decision quality.

The decision process must take account of uncertainty, the values and interests affected, potential harm, reversibility, opportunities for further learning, and the cost of delaying action.

For example, it may be reasonable to accept a less-informed but reversible decision now rather than pursue a more accurate model while irreversible harm accumulates.

That is not abandoning truth. It is recognizing that truth-seeking itself occurs within a changing world and has consequences.

## 5. A possible new foundational principle

I would propose this as a conjecture for Evolution by Emergence:

> The temporal necessity of action
>
> An intelligence exists within a changing reality. It cannot indefinitely defer its participation until its world model is complete or certain. At every decision boundary, it must act—or allow events to proceed without intervention—under the uncertainty that remains.
>
> A functional world model must therefore support not only the detection and correction of errors in its representation of reality, but also the anticipation and prevention of consequential errors through action.
>
> The consequences of those actions become part of the reality subsequently modelled. Learning and acting form a continuing causal loop.

There is a crucial qualification: the necessity of acting does not establish which action is correct. Nor does it establish which values the intelligence ought to optimize.

Those questions remain separate, and we should not smuggle normative conclusions into a theorem about time.

## 6. What this does to our collective intelligence conjecture

Previously, our objective was approximately:

Cooperate to expose and correct errors in our world models.

Your observation suggests a more demanding objective:

Cooperate to identify and correct errors in our understanding early enough to prevent avoidable harm, while improving our ability to act in the future.

It also changes the role of disagreement.

Two intelligences may disagree about a future outcome, agree that an irreversible decision is approaching, and collaborate on the most informative observation they can obtain before that decision.

They need not resolve every disagreement. They need enough understanding to choose their next action responsibly.

And after acting, they can resume correcting their models using what becomes observable.

I think the research question emerging here is deeper than merely finding a stopping rule for deliberation:

Can we establish conditions under which continuing to investigate becomes less valuable than acting with an imperfect model—and conditions under which acting too early destroys our opportunity to learn?

That would connect your work on world modelling to a theory of action, time, and the preservation of future possibilities.

The insight I would preserve above all is this:

The regress ends locally when we must act. But the responsibility to learn does not end there. It continues into the future our actions have helped create.
