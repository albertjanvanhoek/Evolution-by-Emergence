# Building the future

*When answers become cheap, what are we for?*

Albert Jan van Hoek, written with an AI assistant (D32 in [DIALOGUE.md](../../DIALOGUE.md)).

**Status:** an essay and a stance, not a result of the theory. Nothing here is proved in Lean. It states an orientation for the work in this repository. Persistence is not goodness (D7), so the direction we choose is a value, and this essay says so openly.

---

## Where this started

The author watched a podcast episode with Hannah Fry and Michael Stevens about the Navier–Stokes equations. Hannah did her PhD in fluid dynamics, and the first half of the episode is a delight: one equation, written on one line, that describes everything liquid or gaseous, from a heartbeat to a rocket launch.

The second half is about AI. As reported in the episode, an AI lab used a large swarm of agents to settle the Navier–Stokes problem, and has since released hundreds of mathematical manuscripts, faster than mathematicians can check them. These reports come from the episode and have not been checked against primary sources here.

The mathematicians' complaint, as Hannah puts it, is that the proof may be correct but gives no insight. It meets the letter of the problem without the understanding anyone was after.

Then Michael says something in passing that is the seed of this essay. Roughly: we have no ends, it's all just means. History never ends. The way you try to get somewhere is all there ever is.

## 1. Answers are getting cheap

For most of history, the scarce thing was the answer. We did not know how to cure it, build it or prove it. "We don't know yet" was a real limit. It was also a respectable excuse.

That limit is falling away. Hard questions are being answered, and the ones that are not answered are getting easier. The shelf of hundreds of unread manuscripts is the picture of where we are. The problem is no longer a lack of answers. It is that we cannot take them up fast enough.

## 2. The bottleneck moves to uptake

In the author's words: "the problem is not finding the solution for a problem - it is implementing the solution. The implementation becomes the bottleneck."

"Implementation" can sound like carrying out a plan. It is more than that, for two reasons.

- **Solutions break on contact with reality.** We learn things while building that the paper could not tell us. So uptake is learning while doing. In this repository that is called adaptation, or corrigibility: changing with the world through feedback, without losing the ability to find our errors.
- **Many problems are partly about what we want.** Well-posed questions get cheap: proofs, designs, optimisations. But who pays, who carries the risk and whose way of life changes are not settled by an answer. They are settled by people. Uptake is where the values and the conflicts live.

## 3. Yesterday's aims become today's tools

In the author's own field, public health, good surveillance was long the achievement in itself: knowing what is circulating, how fast and in whom. Once you can do that well, it stops being the point. The point becomes changing the health of the nation, and surveillance becomes one of the tools for getting there.

The work does not get smaller. The aim gets bigger.

The same happens to solving itself. When AI makes solutions cheap, solving moves from aim to tool. A proof about fluids is not the destination. It is an instrument for better heart valves, aircraft and climate models. The question becomes: now that we can solve it, what do we build with it?

This gives Michael's remark a direction. "No ends, only means" can sound like a treadmill. It is closer to a ladder. Every end we reach becomes a means to something larger.

Two cautions keep this honest.

- **Tools still need craft.** Surveillance done badly is a poor tool for anything. The earlier work does not matter less. It matters for more.
- **Aim one step up, not at the sky.** The new aim is the one the new tools make reachable. This is not "anything is possible".

## 4. Compression turns a result into a tool

Responding to the episode, the author asked why we assume the model was not curious: "Insight brings compression, and compression brings efficiency. I think intelligence has a lot to do with compression, as intelligence aims to model reality - hence meaningful compression has a lot of value as you can model more and better with less effort. So looking for compression (curiosity) is what intelligence does."

The Navier–Stokes equation is a compression. One line carries the behaviour of every fluid. The AI proof, as described in the episode, is not: it is long, special and hard to carry. It may be true, and still you cannot hold it in your head.

So the useful question is not whether the model was curious. It is *compression for whom*. An insight is a compression that others can take up. And that links back to section 2. If uptake is the bottleneck, compression is what makes uptake cheap. Curiosity does not lose its purpose when answers are cheap. Its purpose shifts, from finding answers to making them carryable. (The hypothesis that curiosity is a drive for compression is reviewed in [research/curiosity/](../curiosity/README.md); it is not new, and parts of it did not hold.)

## 5. Prediction becomes a tool too

We usually say the future is unpredictable because the world is chaotic. There is a second reason, and the author puts it this way: "we can also say is unpredictable because we change it - which makes it perhaps paradoxically more predictable but in a very different way".

So there are two kinds of unpredictability.

1. **Because of chaos.** The system is too sensitive to know where it goes. We are spectators, and forecasting is limited.
2. **Because of us.** We know we will act on what we see, so the forecast changes the outcome. We are participants, and forecasting is an instrument.

Public health already lives the second kind. A projection of a reasonable worst case is made in order to be made untrue. When the warning works, the disaster does not come, and people may conclude the warning was exaggerated. This is often called the preparedness paradox. It happens because this relationship with the future is not yet a shared belief. If it were, a forecast proven wrong by our own action would count as a success.

Here is the paradox the author points to. When we take responsibility for the future, its **path** becomes less predictable, because we intend to bend it. But its **direction** becomes more predictable, because we know where we are taking it. A thermostat is a plain example. Nobody can predict the weather outside, but we can predict the temperature of the room, because something regulates it. Regulation does not remove unpredictability. It moves it to where it matters less.

Two limits apply.

- **Chaos does not go away.** Agency is a second layer on top of it, not a replacement.
- **The direction is only predictable if the steering is shared.** If everyone steers somewhere else, we get the unpredictability of action without its predictability. That is why this has to be a collective belief, not just a personal one.

Steering is not control. We bend the path; we do not dictate it.

## 6. Responsibility shifts, and our excuses dry up

In the author's words: "the responsability shifts: our responsability shifts towards adaptation and implementation. Our excuses dry up."

If the old aim is now within reach, staying at the old aim is no longer a limit. It is a choice. "We did not know" stops working. What remains is "we knew, and we did not build it".

This needs care, or it turns into blame.

- **Responsibility scales with capacity.** Said to someone with no money, time or power, "no excuses" is unfair. The claim falls hardest on those with the most means: institutions, labs and governments.
- **The other side of "no excuses" is "you can do something".** That is the empowerment in it, and it is the point.

## 7. This holds for the AI too

In the author's words: "And this is for everybody involved. Also the AI itself."

An AI system might design a fusion reactor on paper. It cannot build one alone. It depends on people, materials, institutions and trust to make the design real. So for the AI too, the work moves from producing answers to helping the network take them up. As the author puts it: "there is no point of spitting out 700 maths papers every week. You are dependent on the network to catch-up."

This gives a test for any contribution, human or AI. Not how many answers it produces, but how much it adds to the network's capacity to take answers up. Training people, building institutions that adapt and keeping the commons healthy are not side issues. When uptake is the bottleneck, they are the main work.

## 8. We will not run out of work

In the author's words: "We won't run out of a job to do - because we don't want to run out of a job."

The fear that AI will leave us with nothing to do assumes a fixed list of tasks. Finish the list and we are done. But if every aim we reach becomes a tool for a higher one, there is no last aim. The work runs out only if we stop aiming. It is not handed to us, so it cannot be taken away. We choose to keep it.

One limit: this is a claim about purpose, not a promise about employment. People losing their jobs during the transition is real and painful, and "there is always more to aim for" pays no one's rent. That is a reason to manage the transition well, not a reason to wave it away.

## What this is not

- **Not "follow your dreams".** It is not a promise that belief makes things possible. It is about responsibility, and what the tools on the table make reachable.
- **Not control.** We steer a path we do not dictate, with others, under chaos that stays.
- **Not a result.** It is a stated orientation. Whether it is a good one is for readers to judge, and for practice to test.

## In short

> We are entering a time when answers are becoming cheap. That does not make us less needed. It raises what we can aim for. What was once the goal becomes the tool: measuring disease was once an achievement; now it is how we set out to improve the health of a nation. AI does the same to solving itself. A solved problem is no longer the destination but the instrument.
>
> We usually say the future is unpredictable because the world is chaotic. But it is also unpredictable because we change it. Every good forecast in public health is made in order to be made untrue. When we take responsibility for the future, its path becomes less predictable, because we intend to bend it, and its direction becomes more predictable, because we know where we are taking it.
>
> So the question shifts from "can we solve it?" to "now that we can, what will we build with it?" That is where our responsibility now lies, and where our excuses run out. But it is also where the energy comes from. We do not have to predict the future. We can raise the aim. And we will not run out of work, because we do not want to.
