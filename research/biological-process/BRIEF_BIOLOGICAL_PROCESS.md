RESEARCH BRIEF: DOES A REAL BRAIN MEET THE PREMISES OF EVOLUTION BY EMERGENCE?
Separating the biological process from the abstract process the theory describes

Prepared for Albert Jan van Hoek, October 2026

1. Background

Evolution by Emergence (EbE) is a theory of persistence in networks:
https://github.com/albertjanvanhoek/Evolution-by-Emergence
Read CORE.md and entry D17 in DIALOGUE.md, as the object under assessment, not as
evidence. Do not open the folder experiments/ : it holds test material for future
readers.

The theory describes an abstract process: a configuration that exists only while it
pays its upkeep from a gradient, through its connections; that keeps changes which
feedback from outside selects; and that, in a changing world, persists only by
reconfiguring. Its results are machine-checked implications about simple models.
They say nothing about whether any real system meets their premises.

The theory's model of learning came from the brain. CORE.md says: "In a human brain,
learning works mainly by changing the connections between neurons, not by adding or
replacing neurons." The author now separates two things (D17): the abstract process
the theory describes, which is shared by anything that is a process, and the
biological process in a particular brain, which is specific to brain tissue. This
brief asks for the second: what a real brain does, and how far it meets the theory's
premises.

2. Questions

B1. Premise by premise. For each premise below, say what is known about brains, with
    primary sources, and give a verdict: met, not met, or unclear.
    (a) Upkeep from a gradient: the brain's energy cost, and what share goes to
        maintaining connections rather than to signalling. Leads: Attwell and
        Laughlin (around 2001) on the brain's energy budget; Raichle on the brain's
        share of the body's energy.
    (b) Finite capacity and forced forgetting: whether keeping connections is costly
        enough that some must be dropped. Leads: synaptic pruning in development;
        the synaptic homeostasis hypothesis of sleep (Tononi and Cirelli); active
        forgetting.
    (c) Existence through connections: whether neurons depend on their inputs and
        partners to survive. Leads: activity-dependent survival and neurotrophic
        factors in development; dependence on glia and blood supply.
    (d) Selection on cost: whether brains favour cheaper codes or circuits. Leads:
        efficient and sparse coding (Barlow; Laughlin); "neural Darwinism"
        (Edelman), which is contested.
    (e) Learning from feedback from outside: whether learning is driven by error or
        surprise signals that come from the world. Leads: reward prediction errors
        in dopamine neurons (Schultz and colleagues); predictive processing, which
        is contested.
    (f) Retention: how changes are kept, and at what cost. Leads: synaptic
        consolidation; systems consolidation during sleep.
    For each, say whether the evidence is in humans or in other animals.

B2. Where the brain is unlike the model. Check CORE.md's claim that learning works
    "mainly by changing the connections between neurons, not by adding or replacing
    neurons". Leads: adult neurogenesis in the human hippocampus, which is disputed
    (Sorrells and colleagues 2018 against Boldrini and colleagues 2018); structural
    plasticity; neuromodulation; glia. Say also how far artificial networks learn
    the way brains do. Leads: work on whether backpropagation is biologically
    plausible (Lillicrap and colleagues, around 2020).

B3. Other organs, for contrast. Is a kidney a learning process in the theory's sense
    (keeping changes that feedback selects), or only a regulating one? Compare the
    adaptive immune system, which is often described as learning. The point is to
    find where "learning process" stops.

B4. Where the theory does not apply to the brain. List what in a brain the theory
    leaves out or gets wrong. A clear "does not apply here" is as useful as a
    "met".

B5. Measurement. For each premise judged met or unclear, propose how it could be
    measured in a brain, and what result would count against it.

3. Rules

- Check before claiming. Quote primary sources with their location. Search-engine
  summaries are leads, not sources. If you cannot reach a source, say so and mark
  the claim as unverified.
- Mark each claim as established, contested, or your own synthesis.
- Say whether evidence is from humans, other animals, or models.
- Do not stretch the theory to fit. Where the brain does not meet a premise, say so.

4. Deliverable

- A table: premise, what is known, the main sources, and the verdict.
- Short answers to B1 to B5.
- Proposed corrections to "Where it comes from" in CORE.md, quoting the sentence
  and giving the replacement.
- A list of what you could not verify.
