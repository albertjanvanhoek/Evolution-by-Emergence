# Does a real brain meet the premises of Evolution by Emergence?

**A biological premise audit for Albert Jan van Hoek — 10 October 2026**

**Overall verdict — synthesis.** Brains meet several modest premises of EbE: they need continuing energy and material supply, depend on other cells and organs, change through experience, and retain some changes. That does **not** establish the stronger chain that connection maintenance forces forgetting, that learning selects cheaper configurations, or that reliable feedback guarantees continued fit or survival. The evidence supports particular mechanisms at particular scales, not a single validated biological learning loop. The most definite correction to CORE.md is that learning can involve growing new synaptic structures and producing new supporting cells. “Nothing new has to be grown” is false. Adult human hippocampal neurogenesis has stronger positive evidence than the 2018 dispute alone suggests, including studies published in 2025 and 2026. Its quantitative contribution to human learning remains uncertain.

This is a targeted critical review, not a systematic review or a recheck of the Lean proofs. Searches used the named leads, titles, authors and mechanisms, followed by publisher pages, author or institutional copies, PubMed and Europe PMC records and available full texts. I checked newer human neurogenesis evidence through the search date. Several publisher and archive pages failed to load; Europe PMC records supplied the original abstracts in many cases. Each reference below states the access level. Search snippets were used to find sources, not to authenticate quotations.

**Established** means an observed result or well-supported biological mechanism, within the stated species and conditions. **Contested** means a broader interpretation remains unsettled. **Synthesis** means my inference, proposed definition or recommendation. These labels apply to the paragraph or table cell they introduce. “Met” always refers to the specified, limited premise; it does not mean every premise of a corresponding Lean theorem has been satisfied.

The accounting boundary matters. A synapse, neuron, circuit, brain and person are different units. A neuron normally receives metabolic support without individually earning a reward. Organism-level survival, task performance, synaptic persistence and economical coding must therefore be measured separately.

### (a) Energy: what is being maintained?

**Established — human overview and rodent model.** Raichle and Gusnard report the familiar adult estimate: roughly 2% of body mass and 20% of oxygen consumption [2, opening paragraph]. This is a physiological overview, not a new experiment establishing those fractions. Attwell and Laughlin explicitly analyse “**excitatory signaling in the grey matter of rodent brain**” [1, abstract]. Their often-quoted 47% for action potentials and 34% for postsynaptic glutamate effects are model allocations within that signalling budget. They are not measurements showing that 81% of all human brain energy goes to learning, nor a maintenance-versus-signalling split.

**Established — juvenile rat slices; limited extrapolation.** Engl and colleagues inhibited cellular processes and measured oxygen consumption. Cytoskeletal turnover and lipid synthesis were substantial costs [3]. Their Discussion warns that non-signalling processes are not independent of signalling and that young, relatively inactive slices differ from the mature awake brain. This is direct experimental evidence of upkeep costs, with a difficult extrapolation to people.

**Synthesis.** Distinguish three accounts: keeping tissue viable; maintaining and remodelling a circuit; using that circuit. Resting ion gradients enable subsequent signalling. Actin turnover can support both maintenance and plasticity. Calling all baseline consumption “stored knowledge upkeep” would misclassify ongoing neural computation and ordinary cell biology. EbE needs operational categories before it can assign a cost to a configuration.

### (b) Forgetting: an observed outcome is not its cause

**Established — humans.** Developmental anatomy shows “**A phase of net synapse elimination occurs late in childhood**” [4, abstract]. These cross-sectional observations do not identify energy scarcity as the cause, and developmental pruning is not equivalent to adult forgetting.

**Contested framework; established animal findings.** The synaptic homeostasis hypothesis proposes that wake-associated strengthening is balanced by sleep-associated downscaling [5]. De Vivo and colleagues found an approximately 18% smaller axon–spine interface after sleep in sampled mouse cortices [6, abstract]. This measures synaptic size, not 18% fewer memories or synapses. In another mouse experiment, sleep promoted formation and persistence of learning-related spines [7]. The results can coexist: some connections shrink while selected ones grow.

**Established — flies.** Inhibiting Rac slowed memory decay without improving initial acquisition [8]. Forgetting can thus be actively regulated. That does not demonstrate that forgetting is compulsory because maintaining the forgotten trace would exceed an energy budget.

**Synthesis.** Finite hardware does not imply a fixed number of separable memory slots. EbE's budget theorem becomes biologically informative only after identifying a candidate trace, its marginal maintenance cost, the available budget and the relevant retention interval. A fall in recall also needs to be distinguished from loss of storage or impaired retrieval. Evidence of forgetting alone cannot establish the budget mechanism.

### (c) Dependence: real, but not simply reciprocal synapses

**Established — animal and culture experiments.** Ghosh and colleagues found that antibodies blocking BDNF “**reduced the survival of cortical neurons**” [9, abstract], in embryonic rat cultures. Lee and colleagues found that disrupting an oligodendroglial lactate transporter caused axonal damage and neuronal loss in experimental models [10]. The latter paper also reports reduced transporter expression in human ALS tissue; the human observation is not itself a causal intervention.

**Synthesis.** These findings support dependence on partners and supply networks. They do not support a one-to-one equation between synaptic disconnection and death. Removing electrical input, cutting an axon, withdrawing a trophic factor and interrupting blood flow are different interventions. “Connections” must specify which of these is meant. A network that only represents neuron-to-neuron synapses omits necessary support routes.

### (d) Cost selection: efficiency is not the sole objective

**Established framework; animal evidence; computational model.** Barlow's redundancy-reduction proposal concerns efficient sensory representation [11]. Laughlin found a fly interneuron's response matched natural contrast statistics [12]. Laughlin and colleagues estimated ATP costs from retinal biophysics [13]. Olshausen and Field showed that a sparse-code learning algorithm develops receptive fields resembling those of visual cortical cells [14]. None of these experiments measures the general selection of lower-cost brain configurations during a person's learning.

**Contested.** Edelman's neuronal group selection is a population account of development and experience [15]. It is not a demonstrated metabolic-cost minimizer. Crick's critique confirms substantial dispute over the proposal and its relation to existing accounts [16]. Neither treating the theory's name as evidence nor dismissing all competitive plasticity because that theory is contested would be justified.

**Synthesis.** Three hypotheses need separate tests: evolution favours economical nervous systems; development shapes circuits under resource constraints; adult learning favours a cheaper way to perform an unchanged task. EbE's “same job, lower cost” premise belongs most directly to the third. Accuracy, response speed, redundancy and resilience can justify higher expenditure. Lower firing or lower BOLD activity alone is not proof of lower total cost at equivalent performance.

### (e) Feedback: outside outcomes, inside error computations

**Established — humans, supported by primate work.** Schultz, Dayan and Montague relate primate dopamine responses to reward prediction [17]. Pessiglione and colleagues manipulated dopaminergic function and found corresponding changes in reward learning and striatal prediction-error-related activity in humans [18]. This is stronger than merely noticing a resemblance between brains and a learning algorithm.

**Established — rats.** Steinberg and colleagues directly manipulated dopamine neurons. Activation timed to mimic a prediction error changed subsequent cue-related reward seeking [37]. This strengthens the causal case for a specific learning signal, while not establishing a universal rule for learning.

**Contested as a general account.** Rao and Ballard's predictive-coding paper is a model [19]. Keller and colleagues experimentally manipulated visual feedback during mouse locomotion and observed responses to mismatch [20]. Such results support specific predictions; they do not establish that every brain process minimizes one global prediction error.

**Synthesis.** An error is a relation between an outcome and a prediction, not a ready-made label delivered by the world. Its sign and importance depend on internal expectations, bodily needs and learned values. Plasticity during sleep further shows that contemporaneous outside correction is unnecessary for every update [7,23]. External experience can constrain learning over time while internal replay performs some of the updating. Feedback need not be truthful, useful or aligned with survival merely because it originated outside the brain.

### (f) Retention: mechanisms exist; a universal price does not

**Established — animal experiments.** Frey and Morris showed how weakly stimulated inputs could acquire persistent potentiation when proteins associated with stronger stimulation were available; their proposed “synaptic tag” helps explain input specificity [21]. Nabavi and colleagues experimentally inactivated and reactivated a conditioned response using synaptic weakening and strengthening [22]. These support a causal role for synaptic changes, without making a synapse a simple file containing a memory.

**Established — humans.** Rasch and colleagues found that re-presenting an associated odour during slow-wave sleep “**improved the retention of hippocampus-dependent declarative memories**” [23, abstract]. This supports sleep-related consolidation. It does not directly demonstrate that an entire memory moves from one anatomical store to another.

**Established — rats; synthesis about cost.** Suzuki and colleagues disrupted astrocyte–neuron lactate transport and impaired long-term memory formation [24]. Metabolic support matters. Nevertheless, showing that a process needs energy does not measure the additional lifetime energy needed to retain one particular memory. Consolidation, continuing maintenance and retrieval require separate accounting. A mechanism can be essential while consuming a small fraction of the total budget.

## B2. Where biological learning differs from the simplified account

### Does learning mainly change connections rather than replace neurons?

**Established, with a narrower scope.** Human carbon-14 dating and division-marker evidence found no detectable adult production of neocortical neurons in the sampled material [25]. Long-lived neurons can therefore support changing abilities. Animal interventions provide direct evidence that changes in synaptic efficacy and structure participate in learning [7,22].

**Synthesis.** The sentence is a reasonable introductory contrast for adult cortical learning, but “mainly” is not a measured whole-brain percentage established by these studies. It should not imply that connectivity is the only changing variable, that no new cells appear, or that learning only retunes existing synapses.

**Contested, with newer positive evidence — humans.** The hippocampus must be treated separately:

- Sorrells et al. (2018) found no young neurons in their adult dentate-gyrus samples and concluded that adult neurogenesis is absent or extremely rare [26]. Their study included post-mortem and epilepsy surgical tissue.
- Boldrini et al. (2018) reported progenitors and immature neurons across ages 14–79 in healthy autopsy samples [27]. Different material, processing and marker interpretation complicate direct comparison.
- Dumitru et al. (2025) reported proliferating adult hippocampal neural progenitors using single-nucleus RNA sequencing, proliferation markers and computational classification [28].
- Disouky et al. (2026) identified molecular signatures of stem cells, neuroblasts and immature neurons in post-mortem hippocampi [29]. Their Discussion notes that between-sample variability limited quantitative power.

**Synthesis.** The newer studies strengthen the positive case; presenting the field as an unchanged two-paper stalemate would be misleading. But inferred developmental trajectories and marker-defined cells are not a direct longitudinal count of newborn neurons integrating into a living person's learning circuit. The birth rate, functional contribution and clinical significance remain less secure than the existence of supporting molecular evidence. Nothing here makes neuron replacement the general mechanism of human learning.

### What else changes?

**Established — mice.** Learning-related spines can grow [7]. McKenzie and colleagues blocked production of new oligodendrocytes while sparing existing ones and found impaired acquisition of a complex motor skill [30]. Zhou and colleagues showed that manipulating CREB changes neuronal excitability and the allocation of fear memory to particular cells [31]. Thus supporting cells and a neuron's propensity to respond matter alongside connectivity. Dopaminergic modulation supplies another example [18].

**Synthesis.** “Configuration” can include these variables, but expanding the word after every counterexample makes it difficult to test. Specify the state variables in advance: synaptic strength, structure, excitability, neuromodulatory state, glial state and metabolic support. Do not call all of them weights.

### How similar is artificial learning?

**Contested — theoretical neuroscience.** Lillicrap and colleagues discuss possible biological approximations to backpropagation [32]. They explicitly write: “**Performance and representational matches can never on their own establish that backprop-like mechanisms are employed by the brain**” [p. 339]. Their paper argues for a research programme, not a demonstrated identity of learning mechanisms.

**Synthesis.** Brains and many artificial networks share adjustable interactions, distributed representations and history-dependent performance. This is a useful abstraction. Standard backpropagation also requires a specified objective and a method for assigning credit across layers. A brain's local signals, multiple timescales, ongoing activity and changing tissue are not captured by simply saying it updates weights. Artificial systems may also change architecture, memory or external state; neither substrate has one universal learning procedure.

## B3. Kidney and adaptive immunity: where does “learning” stop?

**Synthesis — operational definition.** Call a process learning when experience causes a retained change that alters its subsequent response to a relevant condition, beyond the immediate regulating response. To test EbE's stronger wording, also ask whether feedback selectively stabilizes some changes rather than others. Retention alone is insufficient: a scar records history without necessarily improving function.

**Established — rat kidney.** Nielsen and colleagues demonstrated reversible movement of water channels to and from the collecting-duct cell membrane in response to vasopressin [33]. Permeability changed with the hormone and reversed after withdrawal. This is an excellent example of regulation. By itself it does not show learning.

**Established — mainly mouse kidney, with human comparison data.** Kirita and colleagues found persistent injury-associated cellular states, including failed repair [34]. A kidney is therefore not a memoryless regulator. But a persistent, harmful state does not establish feedback-selected learning. **Verdict — synthesis:** kidney regulation is met; retained biological plasticity is met; a kidney-wide learning loop in EbE's stronger sense remains **unclear**, rather than simply absent.

**Established — mice and humans.** Adaptive immunity is a closer fit. Victora and colleagues experimentally studied selection and clonal expansion of germinal-centre B cells, including the role of T-cell help [35]. Hammarlund and colleagues found long-lasting antibody and cellular immunity in previously vaccinated people [36]. Together these give identifiable variation, outcome-dependent selection and persistence beyond exposure.

**Synthesis.** This qualifies as learning in the proposed functional sense without needing a brain, conscious understanding or an explicit world model. It also exposes a biological difference: immune learning prominently uses cell proliferation and selection, whereas much neuronal learning uses changes within long-lived cells. The shared abstraction is useful precisely because the mechanisms differ. If any persistent response to a disturbance counts as learning, the term will also include damage and will lose much of its explanatory value.

## B4. Where the theory does not yet apply

The following are **synthesis**, distinguishing an abstraction's limits from empirical contradictions.

1. **Survival is not learning performance.** Tissue can remain viable while a particular learning mechanism is disrupted. The cited plasticity experiments measure task outcomes, not whether the brain pays for its continued existence. Mapping reward, accuracy, ATP and survival onto one quantity needs independent evidence.
2. **A synaptic graph is not the complete maintenance network.** Blood vessels, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and the rest of the organism supply essential support [9,10,24]. The graph and accounting boundary must include the relevant routes.
3. **Lower cost is not an observed universal selection rule.** Sparse coding, economical signalling, competitive synaptic change and evolutionary selection are different propositions [11–16]. One cannot substitute for another.
4. **The world does not provide reliable, separately labelled evaluations of every connection.** Credit assignment across interacting neurons is part of the unsolved biological problem [32]. CORE.md's improvements from separately assessable components cannot be transferred to the brain without establishing that premise.
5. **Retention is neither perfect nor necessarily beneficial.** Active forgetting, selective stabilization and state-dependent expression complicate a simple “what works is kept” rule [8,21,22]. What works in a laboratory reward task is not automatically what promotes long-run survival.
6. **Continuous rewiring is not required on every timescale.** Maintaining learned structure and changing it are distinct operations. CORE.md itself allows stable niches and buffers. Its conditional result about coping with changing worlds should not become an unconditional biological law that a non-rewiring brain immediately loses fit.
7. **The scalar budget hides timing and bottlenecks.** A biological account needs delivery rate, local demand, reserves and damage thresholds, not merely an aggregate balance over an unspecified interval. Metabolic support experiments make local delivery particularly relevant [10,24].
8. **Learning without growth is directly contradicted.** New spines and new myelinating cells can contribute to learning [7,30]. “Adapting is cheaper than building” would require a defined comparator and measurements; it does not follow from learning occurring in an existing brain.

Finally, “process” does not by itself mean a self-maintaining living organization. D17 may stipulate that narrower object, but it should name it. Sharing an abstract description with a kidney neither establishes that all processes learn nor explains the specific biology of thought.

## B5. Measurements and results that would count against the mapping

All designs below are **synthesis**, not validated EbE tests. Preregister the unit, time interval, task, support conditions and predicted effect size. A null result counts against a mechanism only if the manipulation worked and the study can exclude the predicted effect. These tests concern biological applicability, not the validity of a conditional mathematical proof.

| Premise | Practical measurements and design | Result against the proposed biological mapping |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) Energy dependence and connection upkeep** | In animal tissue, combine oxygen consumption or ATP sensors with electrophysiology and measured synaptic/cytoskeletal turnover. Compare specific perturbations, with rescue and controls for cell damage. In humans, PET or calibrated metabolic imaging measures regional consumption; it cannot by itself assign cost to an individual trace. | Against a proposed upkeep law: retaining more equally functional connections does not increase measured maintenance cost within the preregistered range, or the apparent cost disappears after accounting for signalling and growth. Sustained viability with no net energy source would challenge energetic dependence itself, but ordinary short survival on reserves would not. |
| **(b) Budget-forced forgetting** | In animals, independently vary learning load and a tolerable metabolic constraint, while following identified spines, old/new task performance and retrieval under matched conditions. Include a metabolic rescue and a control for reduced attention or acquisition. | Forgetting follows interference rather than the measured budget; adequate rescue does not alter the predicted loss; or new learning proceeds without the specific trade-off the budget model predicts. Short-term net synapse growth would refute an immediate one-in/one-out rule, not every long-horizon capacity limit. |
| **(c) Dependence through particular partners** | Perturb a specified input, trophic signal or glial transporter in an animal/culture preparation. Track survival, function and metabolic state separately. Test whether replacing the proposed support rescues the neuron. | The specified dependence is absent, or loss is explained entirely by nonspecific injury. Survival after electrical isolation with chemical support would count against a purely synaptic interpretation, while supporting a broader exchange-network account. |
| **(d) Selection on cost** | Follow learning longitudinally while measuring behavioural accuracy, latency and robustness alongside energetic cost. Compare alternative circuits or codes performing the same task; manipulate the energy price while matching reward and difficulty. | Across an adequately powered test, lower-cost alternatives receive no predicted advantage, or the apparent efficiency gain vanishes when performance and reliability are matched. This would challenge the specified cost-selection rule, not the existence of resource constraints. |
| **(e) External feedback and error-driven learning** | Use contingency reversal and yoked-feedback controls. Fit competing learning models to trial-level choices; record candidate error signals and manipulate them where feasible. In humans, use behavioural and imaging measures without treating BOLD as a direct dopamine measurement. | The proposed error signal can be altered or removed without the predicted learning change, despite effective target engagement. Improvement during replay with no fresh outcomes counts against an every-update-needs-outside-feedback rule, but not against learning from earlier experience. |
| **(f) Retention and its cost** | Follow the same trace-related synapses and behaviour over time. Separate encoding, consolidation, maintenance and retrieval interventions. Combine molecular turnover and metabolic measurements; use restoration of access to distinguish failed retrieval from erased storage. Human sleep-cueing provides a behavioural bridge. | A purported storage mechanism changes without the predicted retention effect; a predicted maintenance-cost scaling is absent; or retained performance depends on compensating processes omitted from the model. One such result challenges that mapping, not the existence of memory. |

**Synthesis — priority.** The most discriminating first study is the budget-forgetting design. Energy use, support and plasticity already have strong evidence. What EbE most needs is a demonstrated chain from a measured resource constraint, through selective retention or loss, to a later functional consequence. Correlations between small synapses, low metabolism and poor recall would not identify that chain.

## Proposed corrections to “Where it comes from” in CORE.md

These are **synthesis: proposed wording**, not edits to the repository.

### 1. Keep the useful contrast; narrow its biological claim

**Current:**

> In a human brain, learning works mainly by changing the connections between neurons, not by adding or replacing neurons.

**Replacement:**

> Many adult human cortical neurons are long-lived. Learning involves changes in synaptic strength and structure, alongside changes in neuronal excitability, neuromodulation and supporting cells. New neurons may also contribute in restricted regions, notably the hippocampus, but their contribution to human learning remains uncertain.

**Reason:** [7,18,22,25–31]. This avoids claiming a measured proportion of all learning mechanisms.

### 2. Avoid identifying capacity with connectivity alone

**Current:**

> Capacity is configuration.

**Replacement:**

> In the abstract model, capacity depends on configuration. In a brain, functioning also depends on current activity, cellular properties, bodily state and metabolic support; these must be specified in any biological mapping.

**Reason:** The original can serve as a modelling choice, but is too strong as an empirical identity [9,10,18,24,31].

### 3. Distinguish external experience from the internal learning mechanism

**Current:**

> The loop that changes it is simple: feedback from outside adjusts the connections, what works is kept, and what is kept becomes material for what can be learned next.

**Replacement:**

> The abstract loop is feedback, change and retention. In brains, external experience interacts with internally generated activity, neuromodulatory signals and several plasticity mechanisms. Some changes persist and influence later learning. Persistence of a change does not establish that it is accurate, economical or beneficial to the organism.

**Reason:** [7,17–24]. This preserves the abstraction without treating it as a complete mechanism.

### 4. Remove the claim that learning requires no new growth

**Current:**

> Because learning rewires an existing web rather than growing a new one, adapting is cheap compared with building: it still costs energy, but nothing new has to be grown.

**Replacement:**

> Learning can reuse existing circuitry, but it can also require new synapses, cellular components and supporting cells. Reusing a circuit may cost less than constructing an alternative, but this depends on the task, timescale and biological mechanism and should not be assumed.

**Reason:** New growth is directly supported by [7,30]; the comparative cost assertion has not been demonstrated here.

### 5. Do not describe social learning as copying a configuration

**Current:**

> And because similar networks are built from the same kind of parts, a configuration can pass from one to another. You watch someone do something, make a few connections, and now you can do it too. Knowledge is infectious.

**Replacement:**

> One person's behaviour can provide another person with information for learning. The receiving brain changes through its own mechanisms; this is not a literal transfer of the first brain's configuration. The conditions under which learning transfers across individuals require separate evidence.

**Reason — synthesis:** The current inference does not follow from having similar cellular parts. This review did not investigate social-learning mechanisms, so a more specific biological replacement would overreach its evidence.

### 6. Make the artificial-network comparison a modelling comparison

**Current:**

> Artificial neural networks were built on this idea, which is why they are called artificial, and they made the structure easy to see: a model's capacities live in its weights, and training is a loop of feedback, adjustment and retention.

**Replacement:**

> Artificial neural networks provide a simplified example in which training changes numerical parameters and can preserve improved performance. Their behaviour also depends on architecture, inputs and state. Similarity at this level does not establish that brains use the same learning algorithm.

**Reason:** [32]. Retain the following existing sentence, “How far they learn the way a brain does is a separate question.” Also retain the paragraph explicitly saying that the brain is an inspiration whose mapping remains to be demonstrated.

## What I could not verify

- **A universal maintenance fraction.** I found no defensible percentage of the whole adult human brain's energy specifically devoted to retaining learned connections, separate from signalling, growth and other cell functions.
- **Energy-forced forgetting.** Pruning, downscaling and active forgetting are verified phenomena. The cited studies do not establish that the maintenance budget makes their occurrence necessary.
- **General cost-driven selection.** The reviewed work does not establish that human learning preferentially retains lower-cost configurations when all relevant functions are equal.
- **A complete human mechanism.** Most causal cellular evidence comes from other animals or isolated tissue. It does not establish the share of human learning attributable to each process.
- **Human neurogenesis rates and causal contribution to ordinary learning.** Recent positive evidence is important, but these studies do not give a definitive functional accounting.
- **Literal biological backpropagation, universal predictive processing, or neuronal group selection as an established general theory.** I verified proposals and relevant experiments, not those sweeping conclusions.
- **A kidney-wide feedback-selected learning loop.** Regulation and lasting injury responses are not enough to establish one.
- **Several full texts.** Abstract-only sources are marked below. Barlow's original chapter body was not retrieved; its author-uploaded record and abstract were accessible, so no quotation is attributed to the chapter body. I did not independently re-run Lean or audit its implementation.

[Author-institution record](https://repository.cshl.edu/id/eprint/15528/). **Abstract; flies.** “Inhibition of Rac activity leads to slower decay of early memory”. Active regulation is not proof of an exhausted budget.

9. **Ghosh, A., Carnahan, J., & Greenberg, M. E. (1994).** Requirement for BDNF in activity-dependent survival of cortical neurons. *Science, 263*, 1618–1623. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.7907431). **Abstract; embryonic rat cultures.** Causal trophic-dependence experiment; quote in B1(c).

10. **Lee, Y., et al. (2012).** Oligodendroglia metabolically support axons and contribute to neurodegeneration. *Nature, 487*, 443–448. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature11314). **Abstract; animal/culture interventions and human disease tissue.** Abstract: “disruption of this transporter produces axon damage and neuron loss”.

11. **Barlow, H. B. (1961).** Possible principles underlying the transformations of sensory messages. In W. A. Rosenblith (ed.), *Sensory Communication*, pp. 217–234. MIT Press. [Chapter DOI](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/9780262518420.003.0013); [author-uploaded record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/243712413_Possible_Principles_Underlying_the_Transformations_of_Sensory_Messages). **Record/abstract only; framework.** Redundancy reduction is a proposed organizing principle, not an experiment on cost-selected learning.

12. **Laughlin, S. (1981).** A simple coding procedure enhances a neuron's information capacity. *Zeitschrift für Naturforschung C, 36*, 910–912. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1515/znc-1981-9-1040). **Abstract; fly sensory physiology.** Abstract: “approximates to the cumulative probability distribution of contrast levels in natural scenes”. Evidence about a specific code.

13. **Laughlin, S. B., de Ruyter van Steveninck, R. R., & Anderson, J. C. (1998).** The metabolic cost of neural information. *Nature Neuroscience, 1*, 36–41. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/236). **Text; fly measurements and biophysical estimates.** Page 36: “Energy consumption is several orders of magnitude greater than the thermodynamic minimum”. Cost estimation is not a demonstration of ongoing cost selection.

14. **Olshausen, B. A., & Field, D. J. (1996).** Emergence of simple-cell receptive field properties by learning a sparse code for natural images. *Nature, 381*, 607–609. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/381607a0). **Abstract; computational model.** “a coding strategy that maximizes sparseness is sufficient to account for these properties”. Similarity to cortical responses does not identify the biological learning rule.

15. **Edelman, G. M. (1993).** Neural Darwinism: selection and reentrant signaling in higher brain function. *Neuron, 10*, 115–125. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0896-6273(93)90304-A). **Text; framework.** Page 115 describes “processes of selection upon variation”. This is the author's theoretical account, not independent validation of it.

16. **Crick, F. (1989).** Neural Edelmanism. *Trends in Neurosciences, 12*, 240–248. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0166-2236(89)90019-2). **Abstract; critical argument.** Abstract: “In this critique I aim to set out Edelman's central ideas”. Establishes a documented dispute; full critique not audited here.

17. **Schultz, W., Dayan, P., & Montague, P. R. (1997).** A neural substrate of prediction and reward. *Science, 275*, 1593–1599. [Author record](https://www.gatsby.ucl.ac.uk/~dayan/papers/sdm97.html). **Abstract; synthesis of primate physiology and computational learning theory.** Abstract: “signals changes or errors in the predictions of future salient and rewarding events”. Not proof of one universal learning signal.

18. **Pessiglione, M., et al. (2006).** Dopamine-dependent prediction errors underpin reward-seeking behaviour in humans. *Nature, 442*, 1042–1045. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature05051). **Abstract; human pharmacology and imaging.** Abstract: “subjects treated with L-DOPA have a greater propensity to choose the most rewarding action”. Supports a particular reward-learning mechanism.

19. **Rao, R. P. N., & Ballard, D. H. (1999).** Predictive coding in the visual cortex: a functional interpretation of some extra-classical receptive-field effects. *Nature Neuroscience, 2*, 79–87. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/4580). **Abstract; model.** Abstract: “We describe a model of visual processing”. Essential qualification when citing it as support for biological predictive processing.

20. **Keller, G. B., Bonhoeffer, T., & Hübener, M. (2012).** Sensorimotor mismatch signals in primary visual cortex of the behaving mouse. *Neuron, 74*, 809–815. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2012.03.040). **Abstract; mice.** Responses were driven by “mismatch between actual and expected visual feedback”. A specific experimental test, not a whole-brain proof.

21. **Frey, U., & Morris, R. G. M. (1997).** Synaptic tagging and long-term potentiation. *Nature, 385*, 533–536. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/385533a0). **Abstract; hippocampal experimental preparation.** Abstract: “the persistence of LTP depends not only on local events during its induction”. Supports stabilization and interactions between inputs; not a lifetime memory-cost estimate.

22. **Nabavi, S., et al. (2014).** Engineering a memory with LTD and LTP. *Nature, 511*, 348–352. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature13294). **Abstract; rodent optogenetic conditioning.** Abstract: “fear conditioning, a type of associative memory, can be inactivated and reactivated by LTD and LTP, respectively”. Causal synaptic manipulation, within an engineered task.

23. **Rasch, B., Büchel, C., Gais, S., & Born, J. (2007).** Odor cues during slow-wave sleep prompt declarative memory consolidation. *Science, 315*, 1426–1429. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1138581). **Abstract; humans.** Quote in B1(f); sleep stage and prior association mattered.

24. **Suzuki, A., et al. (2011).** Astrocyte-neuron lactate transport is required for long-term memory formation. *Cell, 144*, 810–823. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2011.02.018). **Abstract; rats.** Abstract: “Astrocytic glycogen breakdown and lactate release are essential for long-term but not short-term memory formation”. A support mechanism, not a price per memory.

25. **Bhardwaj, R. D., et al. (2006).** Neocortical neurogenesis in humans is restricted to development. *PNAS, 103*, 12564–12568. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0605177103). **Abstract; human tissue dating and division markers.** Abstract: “neurons in the human cerebral neocortex are not generated in adulthood at detectable levels”. Does not settle hippocampal neurogenesis.

26. **Sorrells, S. F., et al. (2018).** Human hippocampal neurogenesis drops sharply in children to undetectable levels in adults. *Nature, 555*, 377–381. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature25975). **Text; human tissue, with macaque comparison.** Abstract: “young neurons were not detected in the dentate gyrus”. Detection limits and marker interpretation are central.

27. **Boldrini, M., et al. (2018).** Human hippocampal neurogenesis persists throughout aging. *Cell Stem Cell, 22*, 589–599.e5. [Text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5957089/). **Text; human autopsy tissue.** Summary: “similar numbers of intermediate neural progenitors and thousands of immature neurons”. Different sampling and methods from [26].

28. **Dumitru, I., et al. (2025).** Identification of proliferating neural progenitors in the adult human hippocampus. *Science, 389*, 58–63. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adu9575). **Abstract; human tissue.** Abstract: “we found proliferating neural progenitor cells”. Important newer positive evidence; functional integration was not directly observed here.

29. **Disouky, A., et al. (2026).** Human hippocampal neurogenesis in adulthood, ageing and Alzheimer's disease. *Nature, 652*, 1264–1273. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10169-4). **Text; human post-mortem multiomics.** Abstract: “its relevance in cognition remains unknown”. Molecular trajectories and cognitive-group associations do not establish causality.

30. **McKenzie, I. A., et al. (2014).** Motor skill learning requires active central myelination. *Science, 346*, 318–322. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1254960). **Abstract; mice.** Abstract: “generation of new OLs and myelin is important for learning motor skills”. Tests production of supporting cells, beyond changes to neuronal connections alone.

31. **Zhou, Y., et al. (2009).** CREB regulates excitability and the allocation of memory to subsets of neurons in the amygdala. *Nature Neuroscience, 12*, 1438–1443. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2405). **Abstract; mice.** Abstract: “neurons with higher CREB levels are more excitable than neighboring neurons”. Excitability and synaptic changes interact; this is not proof of storage independent of synapses.

32. **Lillicrap, T. P., Santoro, A., Marris, L., Akerman, C. J., & Hinton, G. (2020).** Backpropagation and the brain. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 21*, 335–346. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-020-0277-3). **Text; perspective/theoretical proposals.** Quote at p. 339 in B2; discusses possible approximations rather than demonstrating biological backpropagation.

33. **Nielsen, S., et al. (1995).** Vasopressin increases water permeability of kidney collecting duct by inducing translocation of aquaporin-CD water channels to plasma membrane. *PNAS, 92*, 1013–1017. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.92.4.1013). **Abstract; isolated rat collecting ducts.** Abstract: “a reversible translocation of AQP-CD water channels”. A concrete regulation comparator.

34. **Kirita, Y., Wu, H., Uchimura, K., Wilson, P. C., & Humphreys, B. D. (2020).** Cell profiling of mouse acute kidney injury reveals conserved cellular responses to injury. *PNAS, 117*, 15874–15883. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2005477117). **Abstract; mouse experiment, rat/human comparison datasets.** Abstract: “a distinct proinflammatory and profibrotic proximal tubule cell state that fails to repair”. Retained change need not be beneficial learning.

35. **Victora, G. D., et al. (2010).** Germinal center dynamics revealed by multiphoton microscopy with a photoactivatable fluorescent reporter. *Cell, 143*, 592–605. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2010.10.032). **Abstract; experimental mouse immunology.** Abstract: “T cell help, and not direct competition for antigen, is the limiting factor in GC selection”. The selector is itself part of a network.

36. **Hammarlund, E., et al. (2003).** Duration of antiviral immunity after smallpox vaccination. *Nature Medicine, 9*, 1131–1137. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/nm917). **Abstract; human volunteers.** Abstract: “Antiviral antibody responses remained stable between 1-75 years after vaccination”. Long-term retention evidence; immune markers are not identical to guaranteed clinical protection.

37. **Steinberg, E. E., Keiflin, R., Boivin, J. R., Witten, I. B., Deisseroth, K., & Janak, P. H. (2013).** A causal link between prediction errors, dopamine neurons and learning. *Nature Neuroscience, 16*, 966–973. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.3413). **Abstract; rats.** Abstract: “optogenetic activation of dopamine neurons concurrent with reward delivery, mimicking a prediction error”. A causal intervention, beyond a correlation between activity and a fitted error signal.
