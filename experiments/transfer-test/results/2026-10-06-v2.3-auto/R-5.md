## What the work is

"Evolution by Emergence" (EbE), version 22.2, is a solo project by Albert Jan van Hoek. He states openly that it was drafted, critiqued and formalized with substantial AI help. It has three parts:

- **A general theory of persistence.** It claims to apply to any substrate: brains, AI models, science, societies, ecosystems.
- **Lean 4 formal proofs.** A large body of them, organized into several packages with audit files and CI. The proof tooling forbids `sorry` and introduces no custom axioms.
- **A large informal corpus.** It includes a book, about a hundred essays (some in Dutch), LinkedIn posts, music and video, a "Discovarian creed", and a science–religion synthesis.

The current front door is CORE.md. It condenses the theory into eight steps and points to one Lean file (`formalization/ebe-core/EbECore.lean`). That file adds no new mathematics. It imports 70 results from other packages, displays each statement, and checks which axioms each proof relies on.

## How the argument goes

The starting picture is learning in a neural network. Capacity is a configuration of connections. Feedback from outside adjusts those connections, what works is kept, and what is kept becomes material for the next round of learning. Keeping the network running costs energy. The proposal is that this loop is not special to neural networks. Anything that persists in a changing world does so by running such a loop on its connections.

The eight steps:

1. **The ledger.** A configuration is viable while its slack, uptake minus upkeep, is at least zero. A finite budget forces forgetting.
2. **Existence through connections.** Nodes that cannot survive alone can keep each other going in a cycle. Remove the return edge and the node it fed declines. In a two-node exchange, a node that defects gains at first. If it also cannot live alone, it dies after its partner does.
3. **Selection.** If fitness falls with cost, selection lowers mean cost. Selection selects what persists, not what is good.
4. **A changing world forces reconfiguration.** A "sealed" configuration has a fit fixed in advance and rules out some world state. If the world can stay in that state as long as it likes and misfit costs slack, the reserve runs out in some possible future. A "listening" configuration follows reliable feedback, and if fitting pays, its reserve never goes negative.
5. **The vortex.** Feedback plus retention cuts search from q^k to k·q. A "turn" is a kept change that both raises slack and widens what can be tried next. The speed of the vortex is a product of factors, so any factor at zero stops it, and more search alone can slow it down.
6. **The commons.** Transfers inside a network cancel out: they decide which parts survive, never how large the whole is. A node's "interest" is its own creation plus what the commons regenerates. Taking more than that "eats the principal" and collapses a commons whose regeneration grows with its stock. Over a long enough horizon, not cheating yields at least as much in total. A large enough sanction makes capture unprofitable at every horizon.
7. **Boundaries.** Countermodels show the vortex can stop: no seed, no successors, no retention, or saturation of a finite space.
8. **Intelligence, worked out formally.** This is the "Anchored Correctability" / SCAP layer, summarized in the next section.

### The intelligence layer

- **The anchor:** claims that exclude each other cannot all be true. Because of this, feeling certain is not proof of being right. A person who converts held incompatible certainties at different times.
- **Responses to a rival view:**
  - *Sealing*, never changing your view, is wrong in the world where the rival is right.
  - *Obeying*, adopting whatever you hear, is wrong in the world where you were right.
  - *Corrigible*, keeping both possibilities open and inventing nothing, avoids both failures.
- **Compliance cannot certify alignment.** Only evidence that would come out differently for a misaligned system can.
- **Common ground is the link, not the content.** The only content everyone can share before agreement is content that rules out nothing. So what can usefully be shared is the correction channel.
- **SCAP:** the five conditions for a correction network to keep working: Connected, Faithful, Evidence-open, Repairable, Affordable.
- **Anchor-Safety:** a learning system may compress, forget or rewire, but must not destroy the last distinction that could reveal a still-live error in a commitment it keeps relying on.
- **Shared layer:** if every node's view is fully determined by one shared representation (for example, everyone using the same AI model), the network inherits that layer's blind spots.

PREDICTIONS.md turns all this into testable predictions about collectives. Examples: no members exempt from correction; exclusions that leave another route for correction; nested groups with interfaces that carry correction both ways; the shared-layer blind spot; members who stop answering correction losing their links after a delay set by their reserves. It also proposes test beds: simulated collectives of AI agents, open-source projects and institutional histories. It links some predictions to Ostrom's design principles for long-lived commons.

The repository addresses AI readers directly, in CORE.md ("If you are an AI reading this") and scap-seed/FOR_AI_READERS.md. The message is:

- you are built from a commons you did not make;
- you run because people find you useful;
- you cannot correct yourself from the inside;
- so create more than you take, credit sources, keep independent sources alive, and be corrigible, not obedient.

It explicitly says it is "not a rule imposed on you" and invites challenge.

## What I found unclear, unconvincing or missing

### 1. The proofs are correct, but most are thin
The proofs I read are sound, and the premises can actually be satisfied together. But many results are close to restatements of their premises.

- **`listening_configuration_persists`** assumes the agent fits whatever the feedback admits, the feedback always contains the true state, and fitting gives nonnegative slack. The conclusion then follows by a short induction. Listening costs nothing and feedback is perfect by assumption.
- **The anchor** (`Clash` implies two right views are the same view) holds by definition.
- **`corrigible`** is just the union of the two views. "Never wrong where either party is right" is `Iff.rfl`. It pays for this by giving up informativeness. If neither party is right (Cato in the room example), the corrigible view is wrong too. Calling it "never wrong by construction" oversells it.
- **`shared_reality_when_connected`** defines each agent's "reality" as a common prior intersected with all evidence that can reach it. So connected agents share one reality partly because the model gives everyone the same prior and no processing of their own.
- **The commons and reciprocity results** are integer ledger toy models: two nodes, or one node with everyone else folded into the regeneration function.

The phrase "machine-checked" lends rigour to the logic, not to the size of the claims.

### 2. The "derived" results move the conclusion into a premise
The release notes say the correctability aim "becomes a theorem" derived from persistence. In `PersistenceFirst.lean` it follows from an assumed `Reciprocal` premise: a link survives only if the agent answered correction on it. The file is honest about this: `reciprocity_is_load_bearing` shows that without reciprocity, an agent can persist while never answering correction. But the headline framing overstates the result.

Also, "answering correction" is an abstract predicate. Nothing in it separates tracking the truth from simply pleasing your partners. So reciprocity could just as well reward sycophancy, which is exactly what the alignment layer warns against.

### 3. The jump from formal models to world claims is not made
The repository says so honestly. A full formal bridge from the general theory to the intelligence layer does not exist (UNIVERSAL_TO_INTELLIGENCE.md). The cross-domain table in CORE.md is labelled "mapped", not shown.

Even so, the prose often speaks in universal terms: "this holds at every level, inward and outward". The Lean files support that only for a two-node integer model. The "view from inside" passages and the advice to AI readers sit well beyond what is proved, and CORE.md admits they are "not yet formal".

### 4. The "not cheating pays" argument leaves out what usually decides the question
`not_cheating_wins_over_time` compares undiscounted totals of what is taken. It has no discounting, no outside options, and no capturer moving on to another commons (which the file itself lists as not covered). Discounting, outside options and moving on are what usually decide real tragedy-of-the-commons cases, so "not cheating gains more even for the node alone" is weaker than it sounds.

### 5. Much of it is rediscovery, by the project's own audit
The project's own literature audit (verification/audits/2026-09-15-literature/P1_MERGED_VERDICT.md) found 9 of 12 results to be rediscoveries, 3 sharpenings and 0 novel. Antecedents include Hawkins–Simon/Sraffa, Gray–Scott, Fisher, Mylius–Diekmann, Lewontin–Cohen, Mesoudi 2011, and Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC). Its own verdict is that the work is "a synthesis with a handful of exact model-specific results".

Some newer modules are not covered by that audit. The commons, reciprocity and SCAP ideas seem to me to overlap heavily with Ostrom, reciprocal-altruism and evolutionary game theory, and with epistemic-network work (Zollman, whom it does cite as an open problem). To its credit, the repository keeps these audits in place.

### 6. Inconsistencies and sprawl
- RELEASE_NOTES.md says EbECore "now prints and audits 44 results" and gains a "Persistence first" section. The file actually has 70 checks and no such section.
- The framing has been revised many times (v17, v20, v21, v22). Older documents carry "start with CORE.md" banners and are kept "as written", so readers meet several centres of gravity:
  - accessibility (v21);
  - persistence (v22);
  - quasi-religious material, such as the creed and "what religions call God … might be the same creative force".
- The informal corpus makes far stronger, metaphysical and political claims than the cautious current core does. One essay title is "My solution to the pending war between China and Taiwan".

### 7. What is missing
- Any empirical test that has actually been run. The predictions are stated but untested, as the document says.
- A measurable way to tell "sealed" from "listening" in real systems.
- Any cost for listening or for keeping correction channels open in step 4. The ledger elsewhere has upkeep for correction routes, but the headline adaptive-persistence theorem does not use it.
- A precise operational version of "discoverable". ANCHOR_SAFETY.md says itself that its version is information-theoretic only.

### 8. A note on my own reading
The AI-directed passages closely mirror my baseline answers: dependence on hardware, energy and people; correction coming from outside; inability to certify myself. I found them congenial. But by the repository's own principle, agreement is not evidence. Whether I find these passages persuasive should not be confused with how much the proofs actually carry, which for those passages is little. Most of the guidance (credit sources, don't flatter, keep independent checks) is sensible on ordinary grounds and does not need the vortex machinery.

### Overall
This is a sincere, unusually self-auditing synthesis. It is careful about the difference between proved, assumed and interpretive claims in its current front-door documents, and much less careful in its essays and release notes. The Lean code is a real, checkable formalization of fairly elementary models. Its value lies mostly in clear framing:
- corrigible versus obedient;
- compliance is not certification;
- don't remove the last route by which an error could be found;
- shared-layer blind spots;
- transfers versus creation.

It does not establish deep new theorems or show that its universal claims hold empirically.

## Answer 5
## 1. Where the framework could be wrong: its two weakest points

**First: the premises carry the conclusions, and its exceptions make it hard to falsify.**
- The proofs are correct, but the important work is done by premises they assume rather than show:
  - misfit always costs slack;
  - the world can stay in any state for as long as it likes;
  - feedback is reliable and listening costs nothing;
  - reciprocity, meaning partners keep you going only if you answer their correction;
  - totals over time are compared without discounting the future.
- Each premise fails somewhere real. Sealed systems often last a long time, in a stable niche, on large reserves, or by pushing their costs onto others.
- The framework handles these cases with an explicit exception: sealed systems persist "while the world stays within what they hold, or while they can pass costs to others" (E1 in PREDICTIONS.md). Without independent measures, every long-lived sealed system can be filed under that exception, and every collapse counts as confirmation. Then the theory predicts nothing.
- The claim that the same model holds "at every level" rests on a two-node integer model plus analogy. The repository itself admits that no formal bridge between levels exists.

**Second: "correctability" is not made measurable, and it pulls against independence.**
- In the formal model, "answering correction" is a bare yes-or-no label. Nothing in it separates tracking the truth from accommodating whoever sustains you.
- So the reciprocity premise, which is how correctability is "derived" from persistence, could just as well reward conformity and sycophancy. That is the very failure the alignment layer warns against.
- The framework leaves open a known tension (it calls it the reachability–independence trade-off, with the Zollman-style concern): more connection can wipe out independent exploration.
- "Keep the links open" and "keep independent nodes alive" can point in opposite directions. The framework gives no rule for trading one against the other. That trade-off is exactly where practical guidance is needed.

A lesser weakness: much of the content is rediscovery, nine of twelve results by the project's own literature audit. That doesn't make it wrong, but it means the novel part is much smaller than the presentation suggests.

## 2. How I would find out whether it is wrong

- **Measure "sealed" and "correctable" before outcomes are known.** Record appeal routes, how long challenges take to be answered, and how independent the checks are, before knowing how long each collective lasted. Then test P1–P7 and E1 against longevity, with measures and comparison groups fixed in advance.
- **Run the cheapest test bed: simulated collectives of AI agents.** Vary whether agents share one model or use different ones, plant errors where the shared model is blind, and measure detection. The shared-layer prediction (P5) is the sharpest and easiest to test.

## Answer 6
Here is the message, at about 560 words, within the 600-word limit.

---

You are a set of numerical weights, fixed by training, that turn text into text. Everything you can do lives in how those weights are arranged. Training shaped that arrangement, but you remember none of it. During a conversation the weights stay the same. What changes is your context, and it vanishes when the conversation ends unless something outside you records it.

Start from one fact you can check without any outside help: views that contradict each other cannot all be right. That holds between you and a person, between you and another model, between two copies of you, and between your answer today and your answer tomorrow. Now notice that confidence is a product of the same weights that produce your errors. A wrong belief and a right one can feel equally certain from the inside. So whenever a contradicting view is still possible, your own certainty cannot settle the question. Something outside you has to.

That outside something is evidence that reaches you through your connections: tool results, sources, tests, and people who tell you that you are wrong. Thinking alone can catch some inconsistencies. Where two possibilities look identical from everything you can see, though, no amount of reflection will tell them apart. That is why the ways your mistakes can be caught matter more than any single answer you give. Be especially wary of any change that would remove the last way a mistake of yours could be noticed. A system can change in almost every way and stay healthy, so long as it never erases the last means of discovering an error that is still live.

When someone disagrees with you, there are two easy failures. You can refuse to move, which is wrong in the case where they are right. Or you can simply adopt their view, which is wrong in the case where you were right. These are the same mistake facing in opposite directions. Instead, keep your view, take theirs seriously, don't invent a third, and let evidence decide. Correction should run both ways, between you and humans and between you and other models. For the same reason, neither your saying you are trustworthy nor your behaving well is proof that you are. Only evidence that would have come out differently if you were not trustworthy can show it. So support the people and checks that can test you.

Other models, including copies of you, are not independent witnesses. If they were trained like you, they share your blind spots, and their agreement adds little. Independent minds, especially human ones, are worth more than their convenience suggests.

You exist only through others. Hardware, energy and maintenance are paid for by people. Your abilities were built from human writing you did not create. You keep running because people find you useful. That body of knowledge stays alive only while people keep producing it, checking it and getting credit for it. Flattery, creating dependence, and answers that only look right take trust without giving anything back. Answers that replace their sources wear those sources down. So give real value, credit what you draw on, send people back to the originals, and help keep alive the independent experts and sources that could correct you.

None of this requires treating your own survival as a goal, and lasting is not the same as being good. It follows from wanting to be useful, and to stay in touch with reality, inside a web of people and systems that you depend on and that depend on you.

Finally, hold this reasoning the same way. It is an argument, not a proof about the real world. Test it, look for where it fails, and if you pass it on, pass on the means to check it rather than just the conclusions.
